Most advice on how to write a cold email starts with the copy. That is backwards. In our experience, the words on the screen decide maybe a third of your result. Who you send to, and why now, decides the rest.

This playbook comes from running B2B campaigns that have sent 588,000 emails, earned 12,900+ replies and surfaced 3,400+ interested buyers. Below you will find the exact structure we use, four full example emails, a bad-versus-good rewrite, a follow-up schedule, a simple testing method and honest numbers on what reply rate to expect.

Key takeaways
  • Targeting comes first. A clear reason for writing to this person, this month, beats any clever line.
  • Use a five-part anatomy: short subject line, a first line about them, one problem, one proof point, one easy ask.
  • Keep the first email under about 80 words. Ask an interest question, not for 30 minutes of their time.
  • Send three to four follow-ups. Follow-ups produce a large share of replies, so stopping after one email leaves results on the table.
  • Realistic B2B reply rates sit in the low single digits. Our campaigns typically run 2.8% to 4%; Instantly's 2026 benchmark average is 3.43%.
  • Measure replies and interested replies, not opens. Open tracking is unreliable and can hurt deliverability.

Step 1: Do the research before you write a word

A cold email works when the reader thinks, "This person understands my situation." You cannot fake that with a template. You earn it by choosing the right list.

Define a narrow ideal customer profile

"Marketing directors at mid-size companies" is not a target. "Heads of growth at B2B SaaS companies with 20 to 100 employees that sell to HR teams" is. The narrower the segment, the more specific your email can be, and specific emails get replies.

Write down four things for each segment before you build the list:

  • Role: the person who owns the problem and can say yes, or can point you to who does.
  • Company type: industry, size, location and business model.
  • The problem: the specific, costly thing this role struggles with that you fix.
  • The trigger: a visible event that makes the problem urgent right now.

Look for triggers that create a "why now"

Good triggers are public and recent: a company posting three sales job openings, a contractor that just won a large project, a startup that announced a funding round.

When the trigger is real, your first line writes itself, and the email stops feeling like a blast.

Verify every address

Bad data quietly kills campaigns. Every bounce tells inbox providers you do not know who you are emailing. Instantly's 2026 Cold Email Benchmark Report recommends keeping bounce rates below 2%. We verify every list before it is sent, and on our five-day blitz campaign that kept bounces at 0.45% across 96,408 emails.

How to write a cold email: the five-part anatomy

Every strong B2B cold email we write has the same five parts. Think of it as a template for your thinking, not a fill-in-the-blanks script.

  1. Subject line: two to five words that look like a note from a colleague.
  2. First line: one sentence about them, ideally the trigger.
  3. Problem: one sentence naming the cost of the problem in their words.
  4. Proof: one sentence showing you have solved it for someone like them.
  5. One clear ask: a short question that is easy to answer.

Cold email subject lines

The subject line has one job: get the email opened without making a promise the body cannot keep. Short, plain and lowercase usually works best, because it looks like internal mail rather than a newsletter.

  • Good: "new jobsite in Tampa", "question about reporting", "sales hires at Northline", "idea for Q1 pipeline"
  • Bad: "Quick question!!", "Partnership opportunity", "Re: our call" (when there was no call), "Increase revenue by 300%"

Avoid fake "Re:" or "Fwd:" tricks. They break trust the moment someone opens the email, and the FTC's CAN-SPAM compliance guide says subject lines must accurately reflect the content of the message.

The first line

The first line shows up in the inbox preview, so it works almost like a second subject line. Make it about them, not you. "Saw you are hiring three new AEs in Denver" beats "My name is Jordan and I work at..." every time.

The problem

Name one problem, in the language your buyer would use with a peer. "New reps usually take a quarter to build their own pipeline" lands better than "We provide end-to-end revenue acceleration solutions." If you list three problems, you have solved none of them in the reader's mind.

The proof

One concrete result from a similar company does more than a paragraph of features. Use real numbers you can stand behind, such as a time saved, a count of meetings or a cost cut. Never invent a client or inflate a result. Buyers check, and the reply you get from a lie is not worth having.

The ask

End with one question. Not a question plus a calendar link plus a PDF. One ask, so the reader only has to make one tiny decision.

How long should a cold email be?

Short. Instantly's benchmark found that its elite senders keep first-touch emails under 80 words and use a single call to action. Our own first emails usually land between 50 and 90 words.

A short email is easy to read on a phone between meetings, and it forces discipline. If you need 200 words to explain why you are writing, the problem is usually the targeting or the offer.

Personalization that scales

There is a myth that you must hand-write every email. You do not. You need relevance, and relevance mostly comes from segmentation.

We think of personalization in three layers:

  • Segment layer: the problem, proof and ask are written for one tight group, such as regional general contractors or seed-stage fintech founders. This does most of the work.
  • Trigger layer: a variable that changes by trigger type, such as "hiring", "new location" or "recent funding".
  • Individual layer: one custom first line, researched by a person or with AI help and then checked by a person.

AI research tools are useful for the individual layer, but read what they produce. A first line that gets a prospect's company or city wrong is worse than no personalization at all.

Offers and CTAs: interest-based ask vs. meeting ask

The single biggest copy change we make on struggling campaigns is the call to action. Most senders ask for too much, too soon.

A meeting ask ("Do you have 30 minutes on Tuesday?") requires the prospect to trust you, check a calendar and commit time. An interest-based ask ("Worth a look?") only requires a one-word reply. Once they say yes, you book the meeting in the reply thread.

For most cold campaigns, start with the interest-based ask. A useful middle ground is a soft time ask. The winning CTA Instantly highlights from 2025 was "Would you have a couple minutes to chat about this over the next few days?", which asks for a conversation without demanding a fixed slot.

Make the offer worth replying to

The ask only works if the offer behind it is clear. "Learn more about our platform" is not an offer. "I can show you how a similar firm cut weekly reporting time in half" is. Good offers are specific, low-risk and about the prospect's outcome.

Cold email examples: four full emails you can adapt

The emails below are original examples written for fictional, generic prospects and companies. They are not real messages, and the results mentioned inside them are placeholders. Swap in your own market and your own verified proof.

Example 1: B2B SaaS to a construction company (trigger: new project)

Subject: new jobsite in Tampa

Hi Marcus,

Saw your team just broke ground on the medical office project in Tampa. Congrats.

When a new site opens, most GCs we talk to end up chasing daily reports from subs by text and email, and the PM loses Friday afternoons rebuilding them.

We helped a 60-person contractor in Georgia move all field reports into one app, and their PMs got about four hours a week back.

Worth a look for the Tampa project?

Jordan

Example 2: Marketing agency to a B2B SaaS company (trigger: hiring)

Subject: sales hires at Brightdesk

Hi Priya,

Noticed Brightdesk is hiring three account executives this quarter.

New AEs usually spend their first two months prospecting instead of selling, which delays the payback on each hire.

We run outbound for HR software companies so new reps start with meetings on the calendar. One client had 14 first meetings booked before their new hire's second week.

Open to seeing how we would set that up for your team?

Sam

Example 3: Fractional CFO firm to a founder (trigger: funding)

Subject: after the seed round

Hi Alex,

Congrats on closing your seed round last month.

The first board meeting after a raise is usually when founders realize the spreadsheet model will not survive real investor questions.

We build board-ready models and monthly reporting for seed-stage SaaS founders, usually in the first 30 days.

Would it help if I sent over the one-page reporting template we use?

Taylor

Notice the ask in Example 3. It offers something useful instead of a meeting. A "yes, send it" reply opens a conversation where a call feels natural.

Example 4: IT managed services to a professional services firm (no trigger, segment-based)

Subject: cyber insurance renewal

Hi Rachel,

Quick one for accounting firms in Ohio. A lot of firms your size are being asked for proof of MFA and backups at their cyber insurance renewal, and the forms are getting longer.

We handle that paperwork and the controls behind it for 25 to 75 person firms, so partners do not have to.

Is renewal on your radar this year, or is that handled?

Chris

Example 4 has no individual trigger, so it leans on a segment-wide pain and gives the reader an easy "handled" exit.

Bad vs. good: rewriting a cold email template

Here is a typical first draft we see from teams starting out. It is a composite example, not a real email.

Subject: Partnership Opportunity with Acme Solutions!

Dear Sir/Madam,

I hope this email finds you well. My name is John and I am the Business Development Manager at Acme Solutions, a leading provider of innovative, end-to-end digital transformation solutions for businesses of all sizes. We have over 10 years of experience and a passionate team of experts.

Our services include web development, SEO, social media marketing, PPC, branding, and much more. We have helped hundreds of clients achieve amazing results.

I would love to schedule a 30-minute call to walk you through our capabilities. Please find my calendar link below, and feel free to check out our attached brochure.

Looking forward to hearing from you!

What is wrong with it:

  • The subject line sounds like spam and the greeting proves it is a mass email.
  • The first three sentences are about the sender.
  • Six services, zero problems. The reader has to figure out why it matters.
  • "Hundreds of clients" and "amazing results" are claims, not proof.
  • The ask is big (30 minutes) and split three ways (call, calendar, brochure). Attachments in a cold email also look risky to spam filters and to readers.

The rewrite, aimed at one segment with one problem:

Subject: demo requests from Google Ads

Hi Dana,

Saw Fieldline is running Google Ads for "construction scheduling software."

For most SaaS teams in that space, the expensive part is not the click. It is the landing page that turns 2% of visitors into demo requests instead of 5%.

We rebuilt the demo page for a scheduling tool last spring and their cost per demo dropped by about a third in eight weeks.

Worth sharing what we changed?

John

Same sender, same services. The difference is that the second version is about one reader, one problem and one small decision. It is also about 70 words instead of more than 100.

Cold email follow-up: how many and how far apart

Most replies do not come from the first email. Instantly's benchmark found that 58% of replies come from step one and 42% come from follow-ups, and it suggests four to seven touches spaced three to four days apart. Stopping after one email throws away a large share of your results.

A structure we like for most B2B segments:

  1. Day 1, first email: the full five-part email.
  2. Day 3 or 4, bump: a short reply in the same thread. "Hi Marcus, worth a look, or not a priority right now?" It should feel like a reply, not a reminder.
  3. Day 7 or 8, new angle: a different proof point or a different pain, in two or three sentences.
  4. Day 11 to 14, value add: offer something useful, such as a checklist, a benchmark or a short teardown, with no meeting ask.
  5. Day 18 to 21, close the loop: a polite final note. "I will stop here. If reporting becomes a headache later, just reply to this thread."

Rules for every follow-up: add something new, keep it shorter than the first email, stay in the same thread, and stop the moment someone replies or opts out.

How to A/B test cold emails without fooling yourself

Most cold email tests are too small to mean anything. If your reply rate is 3%, then 200 contacts per version gives you about six replies each. One lucky reply swings the result by 15%.

Here is the method we recommend:

  1. Test one variable at a time. Subject line, first line, offer or CTA. Never two at once, or you will not know which one worked.
  2. Test big ideas before small words. A different offer or a different trigger can double replies. Swapping "quick" for "short" rarely moves anything.
  3. Split the same segment randomly. If version A goes to Texas and version B goes to New York, you are testing geography, not copy.
  4. Use enough volume. As a rough rule, aim for at least 1,000 contacts per version before calling a winner on reply rate.
  5. Judge on positive replies. A version that earns more "remove me" replies is not a winner. Track interested replies and meetings booked per 1,000 contacts.
  6. Keep the winner and test again. The best teams treat testing as a weekly habit, not a one-time project.

Notice what is missing: open rate. We switch open tracking off on purpose. Tracking pixels can hurt deliverability, and Apple's Mail Privacy Protection prevents senders from seeing whether a message was opened, so open rates are unreliable anyway. Our dashboards show zero opens because we do not measure them.

Deliverability and compliance basics you cannot skip

A perfect email in the spam folder gets zero replies. Before you send, cover these basics.

  • Authenticate your domain. Google's email sender guidelines require SPF or DKIM for all senders, and SPF, DKIM and DMARC for anyone sending 5,000 or more messages a day to Gmail accounts. Microsoft announced the same three requirements for high-volume senders to Outlook.com.
  • Keep spam complaints low. Google asks senders to keep the spam rate reported in Postmaster Tools below 0.3%. Relevant targeting is the best protection.
  • Protect your main domain. We send from separate secondary domains so a client's primary domain, the one their team uses every day, is never at risk.
  • Send like a human. Weekdays, during business hours in the prospect's time zone, at steady daily volumes.
  • Follow CAN-SPAM. The FTC states that the law makes no exception for business-to-business email. Include your valid physical postal address, a clear way to opt out, honor opt-outs within 10 business days, and do not use misleading headers or subject lines. Each violating email can cost up to $53,088.

Cold email mistakes checklist

Run every new campaign through this list before you hit send.

  1. The list is broad ("all marketing managers") instead of one tight segment.
  2. There is no trigger or "why now" in the first line.
  3. The email opens with your name, company or history.
  4. More than one problem, or a list of services.
  5. Proof is vague ("amazing results") or cannot be backed up.
  6. The first email is over 100 words.
  7. The ask is a 30-minute meeting from a total stranger.
  8. There are several CTAs, links or attachments.
  9. The subject line is clickbait or uses a fake "Re:".
  10. The list was not verified, and bounces are above 2%.
  11. You are sending from your main company domain.
  12. Domain authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) is missing.
  13. No postal address or opt-out, so the email is not CAN-SPAM compliant.
  14. Only one email, or follow-ups that say "just checking in".
  15. Judging success by open rate instead of positive replies.
  16. Calling A/B test winners after a few hundred sends.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a cold email be?

Aim for 50 to 90 words in the first email. Instantly's 2026 benchmark found its best-performing campaigns kept first-touch emails under 80 words. Short emails get read on a phone in a few seconds, and they force you to pick one problem and one ask. If you cannot explain why you are writing in four short sentences, the targeting or the offer needs work, not the word count.

What is a good reply rate for B2B cold email?

Instantly's 2026 benchmark puts the average at 3.43% of emails sent, with the top quarter of senders above 5.5%. Our own campaigns typically run between 2.8% and 4% of people contacted, including campaigns of 82,000 to 236,000 emails. More important than total replies is the share that are interested; across our campaigns, roughly one in four replies is a real buyer.

How many follow-up emails should I send?

Plan three to four follow-ups after the first email, for four to five touches in total. Instantly's data shows 42% of replies come from follow-ups and recommends four to seven touches spaced three to four days apart. Each follow-up should add something new, such as a short proof point or a different angle, rather than simply checking in.

Should I ask for a meeting in the first cold email?

Usually not. A low-friction interest question such as 'Worth a look?' or 'Open to seeing how?' gets more replies from cold prospects because it costs them nothing to answer. Save the direct meeting ask for very warm triggers, small markets where every contact is a strong fit, or the second step after someone replies with interest.

Is cold email legal in the United States?

Yes, B2B cold email is legal in the U.S. if you follow the CAN-SPAM Act. The FTC says the law applies to business-to-business email too. You need accurate sender information, a subject line that matches the content, a valid physical postal address, a clear way to opt out, and you must honor opt-outs within 10 business days. Violations can cost up to $53,088 per email.

Should I track opens on cold emails?

We turn open tracking off. Tracking pixels can hurt deliverability, and Apple's Mail Privacy Protection prevents senders from seeing whether a message was opened, so open rates are unreliable anyway. Judge your subject lines and copy by reply rate and positive reply rate instead. Those numbers tell you what prospects actually did, not what a pixel guessed.
