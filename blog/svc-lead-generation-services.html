How B2B lead generation companies price their services

You will see four main pricing models in the market. None is right for everyone. Here is how each one works and what to watch for.

Pay-per-lead

You pay a set fee for each lead delivered. It looks low-risk, but the definition of a "lead" matters. It can mean anything from a verified contact to someone who replied once. Ask for the definition in writing.

Pay-per-appointment

You pay for each meeting booked. This ties cost to a clear outcome. The catch is quality: some providers book anyone who will take a call. Agree on qualification criteria and on what happens with no-shows.

Monthly retainer

You pay a flat monthly fee for the service, regardless of output. This gives the provider room to test and build properly, and it is common for full done-for-you programs. The risk is paying for effort without results, so look for clear reporting and targets.

Hybrid

A lower base fee plus a performance component per meeting or per deal. It shares risk between both sides and keeps the provider focused on outcomes while covering the fixed costs of tools, data and infrastructure.

Whatever the model, compare total cost against qualified meetings and closed deals, not against leads or emails sent. We explain how our own guarantee and pricing work on the strategy call, based on your market and goals.

How to evaluate a lead generation company: a checklist

Before you sign with any outsourced lead generation provider, including us, ask these questions.

  1. Can they show real dashboards? Screenshots of actual campaign data, with sends, replies and bounces, beat logos and testimonials.
  2. What exactly do they deliver? Leads, replies or booked meetings. Get it in writing.
  3. How do they define "qualified"? Job title, company size, industry and intent should all be part of it.
  4. Whose domain do they send from? If the answer is your main domain, walk away. Secondary domains protect your business email.
  5. Do they verify lists? Ask for typical bounce rates. Ours range from 0.45% to about 3%.
  6. Do they meet inbox provider rules? Google's email sender guidelines require SPF or DKIM for all senders and spam rates below 0.3%. Bulk senders need DMARC and one-click unsubscribe, and Microsoft added similar requirements for Outlook.
  7. Who handles replies? A provider that forwards every reply to you is selling half the job.
  8. How do they report? You should see results weekly, ideally on a live dashboard.
  9. Are they compliant? Ask how they handle opt-outs, CAN-SPAM and, for Canadian prospects, CASL.
  10. What happens if results fall short? Ask about guarantees and how they are measured.

Compliance: CAN-SPAM in the U.S. and CASL in Canada

Cold email to businesses is legal in the U.S. when it follows the CAN-SPAM Act. The FTC's CAN-SPAM compliance guide says the law makes no exception for business-to-business email. Every message needs accurate sender information, an honest subject line, a valid postal address and a working opt-out, and opt-outs must be honored within 10 business days. Our article on whether cold email is legal in the U.S. covers the details.

Canada is stricter. According to the CRTC's CASL FAQ, commercial messages need consent, identification information and an unsubscribe mechanism. Implied consent can apply when a person has conspicuously published their address without a "no unsolicited messages" statement and the message is relevant to their role. Penalties reach up to $10 million per violation for corporations. See how we run campaigns for B2B lead generation in Canada.

We build every campaign around these rules: clear sender identity, a real unsubscribe option, fast opt-out handling, and role-relevant messaging only.
