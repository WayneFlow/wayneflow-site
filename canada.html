Why Canadian B2B buyers need a different approach

Most cold outreach advice online is written for the U.S. market. It assumes you can email any business address as long as you offer an opt-out. In Canada, that assumption is wrong, and it shapes everything from list building to the first line of the email.

The law is only part of it. Canadian B2B markets are smaller and more connected than their U.S. counterparts. A decision maker in Calgary energy or Toronto financial services is more likely to share your email with a peer, for better or worse. Sloppy targeting gets noticed faster.

Buyer behaviour also changes by region. Quebec has its own language law. Western Canada leans on resource, logistics and agribusiness networks. The GTA is dense with finance, tech and life sciences firms that see a lot of vendor outreach. A good B2B lead generation Canada campaign respects those differences instead of blasting one message nationwide.

CASL compliant lead generation: what the law actually requires

Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation applies to commercial electronic messages (CEMs) sent to an electronic address. The CRTC's CASL FAQ sets out three core requirements: obtain consent, provide identification information, and provide an unsubscribe mechanism. It also confirms that messages sent to recipients in Canada from another country must comply.

One detail many agencies miss: the CRTC says messages sent through social media messaging systems, naming LinkedIn messaging specifically, count as messages to electronic addresses. Your LinkedIn outreach is in scope too.

Consent: express vs implied

Express consent means the person clearly agreed to receive your messages, in writing or orally, through an opt-in action. Pre-checked boxes do not count. Express consent does not expire, although the person can withdraw it at any time.

Implied consent exists only in situations the law lists. Per the CRTC's guidance on implied consent, these include an existing business relationship (for example, a purchase within the past two years or an inquiry within the past six months), certain non-business relationships, and conspicuous publication. Implied consent can expire.

There is also a business-to-business exemption, but it requires that the two organizations already have a relationship. A true cold prospect does not meet that test, so we do not rely on it.

The conspicuous publication rule and its limits

For cold B2B outreach, conspicuous publication is usually the relevant path. Consent is implied only when all three conditions hold:

  1. The person published their electronic address, or caused it to be published.
  2. The publication is not accompanied by a statement that they do not want unsolicited commercial messages.
  3. Your message is relevant to their business, role, functions or duties.

The CRTC calls this a higher standard than simple public availability. An address found only through specific queries in a corporate database is not conspicuously published. And if a third party reproduces an address or sells a list on its own initiative, that alone does not create implied consent. The CRTC states plainly that the rule is not "a broad licence to contact any electronic address" found online.

Relevance matters as much as publication. In the CRTC's own example, emailing a published marketing officer about e-marketing training could qualify. Emailing the same company's COO about an administrative assistant course would not.

The burden of proof sits with the sender. The CRTC suggests keeping records such as screenshots, the date, the address and the URL where it was published.

Identification and unsubscribe

Under the Electronic Commerce Protection Regulations, each CEM must name the sender and anyone on whose behalf it is sent, and include a mailing address plus a phone number, email address or web address. The mailing address must stay valid for at least 60 days after sending.

The unsubscribe mechanism must be "readily performed" and stay valid for at least 60 days. Requests must be processed without delay, and no later than 10 business days after receipt.

Penalties

Administrative monetary penalties can reach $1 million per violation for an individual and $10 million per violation for a business, according to the CRTC. The CRTC also notes that a business and the third party sending on its behalf may both be liable, which is why your agency's practices matter.

How we adapt campaigns for Canada

  1. Target published business addresses. We prioritize addresses the prospect or their company published, such as a company website team page, and skip any that carry a "no unsolicited messages" statement.
  2. Match the message to the role. We only pitch a contact on what falls within their job. A VP of Operations hears about operations, not marketing.
  3. Keep source records. For addresses relied on under conspicuous publication, we log where and when each was found, in line with the CRTC's record-keeping guidance.
  4. Identify clearly. Every email names the sender and your company and includes a valid mailing address and contact details.
  5. Make unsubscribing easy. A simple opt-out in every message, processed the same day.
  6. Maintain suppression lists. Opt-outs, bounces and "not interested" replies are suppressed across every campaign we run for you.
  7. Recommend express consent where implied consent is doubtful. If a segment does not clearly meet the conditions, we suggest express-consent routes instead: event follow-ups, content downloads, referral introductions or opt-in LinkedIn conversations. Note that the CRTC says an email requesting consent is itself a CEM, so you cannot cold email someone just to ask for permission.

Frequently asked questions

Is cold email legal in Canada?

Yes, when it meets CASL. You need express or implied consent, clear identification of the sender, and a working unsubscribe mechanism in every commercial message. For cold B2B outreach, the usual basis is implied consent through conspicuous publication: the person published their business address, did not say they refuse commercial messages, and your message is relevant to their role. This is general information, not legal advice.

Can I email a Canadian prospect from a purchased lead list?

Be careful. The CRTC says that when a third party reproduces addresses or sells a list on its own initiative, that alone does not create implied consent. As the sender, you still carry the burden of proving consent. We work from addresses published by the prospect or their company, check relevance to the role, and keep a record of where each address came from.

Does CASL apply to LinkedIn messages?

It can. The CRTC's FAQ says messages sent to other users through a social media messaging system, and it names LinkedIn messaging, qualify as messages sent to electronic addresses. So a commercial LinkedIn message to a Canadian prospect should follow the same consent, identification and unsubscribe logic as an email. We treat our Canadian LinkedIn outreach that way.

How fast must I process an unsubscribe request under CASL?

Without delay, and no later than 10 business days after you receive it. The unsubscribe link must keep working for at least 60 days after you send the message, and it must be easy to use. A multi-step process with logins and buried links is the CRTC's own example of a non-compliant mechanism. We process opt-outs the same day and add them to a permanent suppression list.

Do you write campaigns in French for Quebec?

We can plan Quebec campaigns with French in mind. Quebec's Charter of the French Language requires commercial documents and advertising to be available in French in many situations, and Bill 96 tightened those rules. How they apply to one-to-one B2B email is not always clear, so we recommend French or bilingual copy for Quebec prospects and suggest you confirm the details with Quebec counsel.
