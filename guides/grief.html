Skip to stage five

Denial, anger, bargaining, depression... and finally acceptance: check your offer. When a campaign gets zero replies, most people rewrite the subject line or the first sentence. Usually the real problem is the offer: what you're asking people to say yes to.

Here's how big the difference can be. One agency, RevGrowth, shares typical lead rates by offer type: a great offer books about 1 lead per 25 people (their range for incredible offers is 1 per 25 to 200), while a hard offer (their examples: SEO, cybersecurity, recruiting) books about 1 lead per 10,000 (their range is 1 per 1,000 to 10,000). Those are one agency's numbers, not a promise, but they show why the offer is the first thing to test.

Fix the offer first. Then the copy has something worth selling.

Step 1: Write 3 different offers

Three offers that are really different, not three wordings of the same thing. Use this formula for each one:

Offer formula
For [who exactly]
we'll [what you give or do]
so they get [one result they care about]
in [time frame]
with [what makes it low risk: free / guarantee / small first step].
The ask: [one small yes, e.g. "want me to send it?"]
  • Make them differ on what they get. For example: one free thing, one small paid step, one guarantee.
  • Keep the ask small. "Want me to send it?" is easier to say yes to than "Can we book 30 minutes?"
  • Only promise what you can deliver. A guarantee you can't keep is worse than no offer.

The 6 offer examples

Offer typeExample lineWorks well when
1. Free teardown"I recorded a 3-minute video on 2 things I'd fix on your [website / ads / funnel]. Want me to send it?"You can spot problems from the outside

3. Pilot with a guarantee"Try it for 30 days on one [location / product]. If you don't get [result], you don't pay."You're confident in the result and can afford a few refunds

6. Small first step"A one-week [setup / fix] for a flat [$X], before any bigger commitment."Your main offer is expensive or a big decision
Real only: Offer 5 only works with a real result. Never round up or invent a number.

Step 2: Set up a fair test

  1. One list, split 3 ways at random. Same kind of people in each group, so the only difference is the offer.
  2. Same copy structure. Same length, same subject style, same follow-up count. Only the offer line changes.
  3. Same inboxes and days. Send all three at the same time from the same mix of inboxes.
  4. Same definition of a lead. Decide now what counts: a reply saying "interested", "send it" or "let's talk". "No thanks" and "unsubscribe" are replies, not leads.

Step 3: How many people per offer

Send each offer to at least 300 people, and ideally 500, with every person getting your full sequence. Here's why: if an offer gets 1 lead per 100 people, 300 people gives you about 3 leads, which is the smallest number you can start to trust. Fewer people, and one lucky reply can fool you.

How long it takes depends on your sending: new people per day = inboxes × 30 ÷ emails in your sequence. With 5 inboxes and a 3-email sequence, that's 50 new people a day, so 1,500 people takes 30 working days. Then wait about a week after the last follow-up before you judge.

Step 4: Read the result (contacts per lead)

One number tells you which offer wins: contacts per lead = people contacted ÷ leads. Lower is better. It's better than reply rate, because "no thanks" counts as a reply but isn't worth anything.

Step 5: Keep the winner, test again

  • Clear winner (best offer has 3+ leads and needs about half the contacts per lead of the next one): scale it.
  • Close call: send another 300 people to the top two before deciding.
  • All three at zero after 500 people each: the offer isn't the only problem. Check the list (right people?) and deliverability (landing in spam?) before writing new offers.
  • Every month: keep the winner running and test one new challenger offer against it.

Quick answers

What counts as a lead?

A reply that shows real interest: "send it", "tell me more", "let's talk". Decide this before you start and use the same rule for all three offers.

My list is only 300 people. Can I still test?

Split it into 3 groups of 100. The result is less certain, so treat it as a hint and confirm the winner on your next list.

Is a free offer always best?

No. Free can bring lots of replies from people who never buy. Look at contacts per lead, and later at which leads turn into calls and deals.

How often should I test new offers?

Keep your winner running and test one new offer against it each month.
