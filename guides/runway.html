The short answer

Lose 2 clients at $2,500 each and $5,000 a month is gone. That's what a thin client list really costs. Your runway is only as long as your pipeline is full.

This checklist gives you three numbers: how much one lost client costs you, how many new clients you need each month just to stay level, and how many people your outreach has to reach to replace them before the money runs out.

Why a thin client list is risky

Clients always leave. Projects end, budgets get cut, a new boss brings their own agency. You can't stop every exit. You can only make sure each exit isn't a crisis.

Replacing a client takes time. If you start looking only after a client leaves, the gap is already open. A new cold email setup needs new inboxes warmed up first: Instantly's 2026 benchmark report suggests starting at 5 to 10 emails a day and building up over 4 to 6 weeks. Then the replies, calls and proposals take more weeks. Those weeks come out of your runway.

Referrals don't come on schedule. They're great clients, but you can't turn them up when two clients leave in the same month. A steady outbound channel is the part you control.

The setup checklist

The three steps from the reel. Each has the why, then exactly how.

Step 1: Know what each client is worth and how long you can last

Why: you can't plan for a loss you haven't measured. Most owners know their revenue, but not what share each client carries or how many months of cash they have.

  1. List every client with their monthly fee.
  2. Share of revenue = client fee ÷ total monthly revenue. Mark any client that's a big share; losing them is the biggest hit.
  3. Monthly costs: payroll, tools, rent, your own pay. Everything that leaves each month.
  4. Gap after a loss = monthly costs − revenue left after the loss.
  5. Runway = cash in the bank ÷ gap after a loss. That's how many months you have to replace the client.

Step 2: Know how many clients you need each month to stay level

Why: if clients leave at a steady pace, you need new ones at the same pace just to stand still. Growth only starts after that number.

  1. Average client stay in months. Look at your past clients. If you're new, use a careful guess and update it later.
  2. Stay-level number = clients you have now ÷ average stay in months. That's how many clients you lose, and must replace, in a normal month.
  3. Growth number = stay-level number + the extra clients you want each month.
  4. Round up. You can't sign half a client.

Step 3: Build a pipeline that books calls every week

Why: your stay-level number only works if the calls behind it show up every week, not when you remember to look for them.

  • Work backwards from your growth number to calls, people and emails (the math is below).
  • Don't rely on referrals alone. Keep them, and add one outbound channel you control.
  • Send from separate domains, never your main one. Hunter found custom domains got 5.2% replies vs 2.5% for free Gmail-style addresses (State of Email Outreach 2026).
  • Email 1 or 2 people per company. Hunter found that got 5.1% replies vs 3.5% for 3 or more.
  • Keep bounces under 2% (Instantly) by verifying every list.
  • Send every working day. Google's sender guidelines say to send at a consistent rate and avoid bursts.
  • Start before you need the clients. With 4 to 6 weeks of warm-up, the best time to build is while things are fine.

Worked example

An agency has 6 clients at $2,500 a month = $15,000 a month. Monthly costs are $12,000. There's $10,000 in the bank. Clients stay 12 months on average. These are example numbers; swap in your own.

Now the pipeline. The owner wants to replace both lost clients in the next 2 months, so they need 1 new client a month, plus 0.5 a month to stay level. That's 1.5, rounded up to 2 clients a month.

Your ratio will be different: About 100 people per opportunity came from one small pilot. Your list, offer and market will change it. Use your own number after your first full month of sending.

Common mistakes

  • Not knowing the runway number. If you don't know how many months you have, every loss feels like an emergency.
  • Building the pipeline only after a client leaves. Warm-up alone takes weeks.
  • Counting on referrals to fill the gap. They come when they come, not when you need them.
  • Planning only to replace, never to grow. The stay-level number keeps you flat. Growth starts above it.
  • Using someone else's ratios forever. Borrow a ratio to start, then use your own.

Quick answers

How many months of runway is safe?

There's no single right number. What matters is that your runway is longer than the time it takes you to replace a client, including warm-up, booking calls and closing. If replacing a client takes you 3 months, a 2-month runway is a problem.

I don't know my average client stay. What do I use?

Look at your past clients and average how many months each stayed. If you're new, make a careful guess, then update it every quarter as clients start and end.

Should I stop taking referrals?

No. Keep every referral. Just don't make them your only source of new clients, because you can't turn them up when you need them.

When should I start cold email?

Before you need it. Warm-up alone takes about 4 to 6 weeks, so the best time to start is while your client list is still full.
