The Day-One Shortlist Checklist: Get on the Buyer's List Before They Call Sales

Why the shortlist is decided before you show up

In the 6sense 2025 Buyer Experience Report (more than 4,000 B2B buyers in North America, EMEA and APAC), 94% of buying groups ranked their preferred vendors before first contact with any seller. They then bought from that early favorite 77% of the time.

6sense also found that buyers put about four of the five vendors they evaluate on their shortlist from day one. So if you get added late, you are fighting for one open seat, against a favorite that already has a head start.

The lesson is simple. By the time a buyer fills in your contact form or answers your email, most of the decision is already made. You win by being known before they start looking.

What "day one" really means

Day one is the moment a buyer writes down the names they will look at. It usually happens in their head or in a quick team chat: "Who do we know that does this?" The names that come up are the ones they've seen often, recently, and in a useful way.

  • Often: they've seen you more than once. One email or one post is easy to forget.
  • Recently: they've seen you in the last few weeks, not last year.
  • Useful: what they saw taught them something or solved a small problem.

The 12-point day-one shortlist checklist

Go through each point and mark it yes or no. Every "no" is a reason a buyer might not think of you on day one.

#CheckYes / No
1You have a written list of the 200 to 500 companies most likely to buy in the next 12 months
2You know the 2 to 3 job titles inside those companies who start the search
3Every company on the list hears from you at least once a month (email, post or both)
4You post something useful at least once a week, on the channel your buyers actually use
5Your cold emails teach or offer something. They don't only ask for a meeting
6Your website says in one line who you help and what result you get
7Your website shows a price range or "from" price, so you're not cut for being a mystery
8You have at least 2 case studies with real numbers from your buyers' world
9You have a page comparing you to the usual alternatives (including doing nothing)
10You're listed on the review or directory sites your buyers check
11When someone types your category plus your niche into Google or an AI chat, you show up somewhere
12You track which target companies reply, visit or engage, so you know who is warming up
Score it: 10 to 12 yes: you're in good shape. 6 to 9: pick the 2 easiest gaps and fix them this month. Under 6: start with points 1, 3 and 4. Without a list and a steady rhythm, the rest won't matter.

How to fix each part

Build the future-buyer list (points 1 and 2)

Your future buyers are companies that fit your best clients but aren't looking yet. Pull them from the same industry, size and region as your 3 best clients. Add the 2 or 3 job titles that usually start the search. This list is who you show up for, every week.

Show up on a rhythm (points 3, 4 and 5)

One email is a message. Twelve useful touches over a few months is a brand. Mix cold email with posts so the same name shows up in two places. Keep every touch useful: a short tip, a number, a checklist, a case study.

Make your website easy to shortlist (points 6 to 9)

When a buyer builds a list, they skim websites fast. If they can't tell who you help, what it costs or whether it worked for someone like them, they move on. Put those three answers on your homepage or one click away.

Be findable when they search (points 10 and 11)

Buyers check review sites, Google and AI chat tools before they call anyone. Search for your own category the way a buyer would. If you're not there, get listed, ask happy clients for reviews, and publish pages that answer the exact questions buyers ask.

The weekly plan

  1. Monday: pick one useful idea for the week (a tip, a number, a mistake you see often).
  2. Tuesday: post it on the channel your buyers use.
  3. Wednesday: send a short cold email to the next batch of your future-buyer list, built on the same idea.
  4. Thursday: reply to every response the same day. Note who is "not now" and when to come back.
  5. Friday: check point 12. Which target companies engaged this week? Move them to a warmer list.

Worked example

Rough math to show why day one matters. Say 100 companies in your market start buying this year. These are example numbers, using the 6sense rates above:

  1. 94 of the 100 (94%) will rank their favorites before they talk to anyone.
  2. If you are the early favorite in 10 of those deals, at a 77% win rate you'd expect about 7.7 wins (10 × 0.77).
  3. If you are a late add in 10 deals instead, the other 23% of wins gets shared by the other vendors. With about 4 others, that's roughly 5.75% each (23 ÷ 4), or about 0.6 wins (10 × 0.0575).
  4. Same 10 deals. About 7.7 wins as the favorite vs about 0.6 as a late add.

The exact numbers will be different for you. The gap is the point: being early is worth more than being better on the sales call.

Quick answers

If buyers pick favorites before contact, is cold email useless?

No. Cold email is one of the ways to become the favorite before they start looking. It works best when it's steady and useful, not one blast.

How long does it take to get on day-one shortlists?

It depends on your market and how long buyers take to decide. Plan on months, not weeks. That's why the rhythm matters more than any single email.

What if I'm already a late add to a deal?

Ask what they liked about the vendors already on the list and what's missing. Then show one clear way you're different. You can still win, but the odds are lower.

Do I need to post on social media to do this?

It helps, because buyers see your name in two places. If you can only do one thing, send steady, useful emails to your future-buyer list.
