Why the first line matters so much

Most inboxes show the subject and the first line before the email is even opened. That preview is the whole first impression. If it reads like every other pitch, people archive it without opening.

It also matters because the first email does most of the work. Instantly's 2026 benchmark report found that 58% of all replies come from the first email in a sequence. And in Hunter's 2026 report, emails with two personal details in the body got 5.6% replies vs 3.6% with none.

5 opener formulas that work

Every good opener does one thing: it shows you know something real about them, and it leads naturally to why you're writing.

1. The trigger

Something just changed at their company: hiring, a new office, new funding, a new product, a new leader. Changes create new problems, which makes them a natural reason to write.

Saw you're hiring 3 sales reps in Austin. Usually that means the pipeline has to grow fast too.

2. Their own words

Point to something they posted, said on a podcast or wrote on their blog. Mention one specific idea, not just "loved your post".

Your LinkedIn post about losing deals to slow follow-up stuck with me. That's exactly where we work.

3. The peer result

Show a real result for a company like theirs. Only use results you can prove.

We helped another construction software company book 31 qualified demos from cold email.

4. The specific observation

Something you noticed on their website, reviews or ads that points to a real problem you can fix. Say it kindly.

5. The honest question

A short question about how they do something today. It works best when your list is tightly targeted, so the question is clearly relevant.

Where to find something specific in 2 minutes

  • Job posts on their careers page or LinkedIn: who they're hiring tells you what they're trying to grow.
  • Their LinkedIn posts and the founder's posts from the last 30 days.
  • News or press page on their site: launches, awards, new locations.
  • Google reviews (great for local businesses): what customers praise or complain about.
  • Their website: new services, pricing page, how people contact them.
  • Podcasts or interviews: search the founder's name plus "podcast".
At scale: You don't need a new detail for every person. Group people who share the same trigger (for example, every company hiring sales reps this month) and write one strong opener for the whole group. Hunter's data shows small groups of 21 to 50 people get the best reply rates anyway.

The 4-question test for every opener

  1. Could I send this exact line to someone else? If yes, it's not specific enough. Rewrite it.
  2. Is it true and recent? A 2-year-old "congrats on the launch" hurts more than it helps.
  3. Is it about them, not me? The first word should usually be about their business, not "I" or "we".
  4. Does it lead to my reason for writing? The second line should connect the detail to the problem you solve.

Quick answers

Isn't it rude to skip the greeting?

Keep "Hi Sarah," and go straight to something about them. That's friendly, and it gets read.

Can AI write my openers?

Yes, if you give it real facts to work with and tell it not to invent anything. AI openers fail when they're built from a name and job title only, because they turn into empty compliments.

How long should the first line be?

One short sentence, ideally under 20 words, so it fits in the inbox preview.

What if I can't find anything about a prospect?

Use a peer result or an honest question for their industry, or leave them out of this campaign. A tighter list usually beats a bigger one.
