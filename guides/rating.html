Why rate your own cold emails

Most people judge their cold email by one question: is it polite? That's the wrong test. The reader is a stranger. They give your email a few seconds, often on a phone. They don't care if it's polite. They care if it's about them and if it's easy to answer.

The people who get these emails say the same thing. In Hunter's State of Email Outreach 2026, 65% of decision makers said cold emails fail because they feel too sales-focused. 61% said they fail because they aren't relevant. A score from 1 to 10 makes those two problems easy to see before you hit send.

This guide gives you the exact scorecard from the reel, the score (and the reason) for each bad email in it, the 10/10 structure, and a prompt that rates and rewrites any email for you. All companies in the examples are made up.

The scorecard: 5 checks, 10 points

Score every first email on these 5 checks. Add up the points. The weights are not equal on purpose: the line about them is worth the most, because it decides if the rest gets read.

Red flags: the score can't go above 2

Some mistakes sink an email no matter how good the rest is. If you see any of these, score the email as normal, then cap it at 2.

  • No subject line. It looks like a mistake or a trick.
  • Hype: ALL CAPS, "FREE", "!!!", "Act now".
  • A follow-up that adds nothing new, like "Just bumping this".
  • A fake "Re:" or "Fwd:" on a first email.
  • Any fact you can't back up. A made-up result is worse than no result.

The emails from the reel, scored

"I hope this finds you well": 1/10

1/10 (39 words)
Hi John,

I hope this email finds you well. My name is Dan and I'm with Apex Growth. We are a leading full-service marketing agency that helps businesses grow.

Would you be open to a quick call this week?

Score: Them 0, Problem 0, Proof 0, Ask 0, Short 1 = 1/10. It's polite, and that's all it is. Three lines in a row are about the sender: the greeting, the name and "a leading agency". In the inbox preview, the reader sees "I hope this email finds you well" and knows it's a pitch before opening it. Our WELL guide has 5 openers to use instead.

The 180-word email: 2/10

2/10 (180 words)
Hi Sarah,

I hope you're having a great week. My name is Dan, and I'm the founder of Apex Growth, a full-service marketing agency based in Chicago. Over the past eight years, we have partnered with more than 40 companies across many industries, helping them grow their brand, reach new customers and increase revenue.

Our team offers a wide range of services, including SEO, paid ads, social media management, email marketing, content writing, website design and conversion rate optimization. We take a data-driven approach to everything we do, and every client gets a dedicated account manager and monthly reports.

I came across your company and was impressed by what you've built. I believe there could be a great fit between our two companies, and I would love the chance to learn more about your goals for next year and share some ideas on how we could help you reach them faster.

Would you be open to a 30-minute call next week? Here is my calendar link so you can pick a time that works best for you.

Best regards,
Dan

Score: Them 1, Problem 0, Proof 1, Ask 0, Short 0 = 2/10. Nobody reads this on a phone. It lists 7 services, says "we" over and over, and only mentions the reader in one vague line. Then it asks a stranger for 30 minutes.

The data backs this up. In The Ultimate Cold Email Data Report by 30 Minutes to President's Club and Gong (85M+ cold emails), emails of 51 to 100 words got a 2.6% reply rate. Emails of 151 to 200 words got 1.6%. Hunter's study of 34 million cold emails found 20 to 39 words had the highest average reply rate, at 4.5%. Aim for 75 words or less. "Opportunity" + "30-minute call?": 4/10

4/10 (41 words)
Subject: Opportunity

Hi Lisa,

Saw Brightway is hiring two sales reps. I have an exciting opportunity to share with you. Our platform helps teams grow revenue faster, and over 200 companies use it.

Do you have 30 minutes next Tuesday for a call?

Score: Them 2, Problem 0, Proof 1, Ask 0, Short 1 = 4/10. This one has a real fact (the hiring) and it's short. But the fact doesn't lead anywhere, "opportunity" is a word every pitch uses, and the ask is 30 minutes of a stranger's week.

The ask matters a lot. The same 85M-email report found that asking for a meeting cut reply rates by 44%, while making an offer lifted them by 28%. Their advice: ask for interest, or offer something useful they get even if they never buy, like an audit or a short report. Hunter's survey points the same way: decision makers prefer "Can I send more info?" and "Open to learning more?" over booking a meeting up front.

"Loved your post! Same college!": 3/10

3/10 (34 words)
Hi Mike,

Loved your post on LinkedIn! Also saw we both went to Ohio State. Go Bucks!

We help software companies with outbound. We've worked with 50+ SaaS teams.

Free for a call Thursday?

Score: Them 1, Problem 0, Proof 1, Ask 0, Short 1 = 3/10. It feels personal, but it isn't relevant. Which post? What about it? And the college has nothing to do with outbound. Personal is not the goal. Relevant is.

The 30MPC and Gong report says it directly: "Executives don't care if you reference their alma mater." In its ranking of 5 kinds of personalization, activity-based details (what their company is doing) scored highest at 24%. Details about the person scored 6%, and no personalization scored 2%.

"Just bumping this": 2/10

2/10 (13 words)
Hi Mike,

Just bumping this to the top of your inbox. Any thoughts?

Score: Them 0, Problem 0, Proof 0, Ask 1, Short 1 = 2/10, and it's a red flag. Short follow-ups are good. The 30MPC report found bump emails of 1 to 2 sentences got about 2x the replies of longer ones. The problem here is that it adds nothing. It's about your email, not their business. Keep it short, but bring one new thing: a new angle, a new proof, or the same ask said a new way.

The 10/10 email: 4 parts

A 10/10 email doesn't need to be clever. It needs 4 parts, in this order, and nothing else.

  1. 01 Name: use their name, then go straight to them. No "I hope" line.
  2. 02 Business: one true, recent fact about their business, and the problem that usually comes with it. This is where "personal" becomes "relevant".
  3. 03 Proof: one specific result for a company like theirs. Only results you can back up.
  4. 04 One-word ask: one small question they can answer with "yes". "Worth a look?" is the classic.
10/10 example (38 words)
Subject: the Denver office

Hi Lisa,

Congrats on the second office in Denver. A new office usually needs a full sales calendar fast.

A staffing firm in Austin booked 25 sales calls in its first month with cold email.

Worth a look?

Score: Them 3, Problem 2, Proof 2, Ask 2, Short 1 = 10/10. The first line is a real fact about Lisa's business. The second line is the problem that comes with it. The third is a result for a company like hers. The ask takes one word to answer. It's also written about her: count the "you" and "your" versus the "we" and "our". The 30MPC report found top reps use "you", "your" and "your team" 29% more often than average reps.

Worked example: from 2/10 to 10/10

Here is a typical first email from a made-up IT company to the owner of a law firm. Word counts are for the body, from "Hi" to the end.

Before: 87 words
Subject: Quick question about IT

Hi Tom,

I hope you're doing well. I came across Miller Law on LinkedIn and wanted to reach out. We are Northside IT, a managed IT provider with over 15 years of experience. We offer help desk support, cloud backups, cybersecurity, Microsoft 365 setup and phone systems. Many professional firms trust us to keep their technology running smoothly. I'd love to set up a 30-minute call to learn about your current setup and show you how we can help. Are you free next Wednesday or Thursday afternoon?

Check the math: before is 1 + 0 + 1 + 0 + 0 = 2. After is 3 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 10. Now fix it one check at a time, lowest score first.

  1. Fix the ask (0 → 2). Delete the 30-minute call and the two days. Write "Worth a look?"
  2. Fix Them (1 → 3). Spend 2 minutes on their website. Tom's firm just opened a Tampa office. Lead with that.
  3. Fix the problem (0 → 2). What comes with a second office? Two sets of laptops, logins and backups to keep safe. That's one sentence.
  4. Fix the proof (1 → 2). Swap "15 years" and "many firms trust us" for one result: a 20-person law firm moved both offices to one secure setup in 3 weeks.
  5. Fix the length (0 → 1). Delete the service list and "I hope you're doing well". The service list is the biggest cut.
  6. Fix the subject. "Quick question about IT" becomes "the Tampa office". In Hunter's 2026 data, subject lines like "Quick question" got a 2.5% reply rate vs 2.9% for other subject lines.
After: 50 words
Subject: the Tampa office

Hi Tom,

Saw Miller Law just opened a Tampa office. Two offices usually means two sets of laptops, logins and backups to keep safe.

We helped a 20-person law firm in Orlando move both of its offices to one secure setup in 3 weeks, with no downtime.

Worth a look?

The new email is 37 words shorter (87 − 50 = 37). Nothing important was lost. The help desk, the backups and the phone systems still exist. They just wait for the reply.

Common mistakes when scoring

  • Grading your own email too kindly. Read it as the stranger would, on a phone, in 5 seconds. If you have to explain a line, it scores lower.
  • Counting a compliment as "Them". "Loved your post" is a 1. A fact about their business that leads to your reason is a 3.
  • Thinking a short email is automatically good. Short is only 1 point out of 10. A 15-word email with no reason to care still scores 1.
  • Adding proof you can't back up. If you don't have a real result yet, score Proof 0 and use a free offer as your ask instead.
  • Scoring one email and copying it to 1,000 people. The "Them" line has to be true for every person who gets it. Group people who share the same fact (like everyone hiring sales reps this month).

The 1-10 rating prompt
You are a strict cold email reviewer. Rate my cold email from 1 to 10 using this scorecard:

1. Them (0-3): 0 = about me, a pleasantry or "Hi there". 1 = only their name or company, a compliment, or a personal fact (a post, a college). 2 = a real fact about their business that doesn't lead anywhere. 3 = a specific, recent fact about their business that leads to why I'm writing.
2. Their problem (0-2): 0 = no reason to care. 1 = a general industry problem. 2 = a problem that follows from the first line.
3. Proof (0-2): 0 = none or hype. 1 = a general claim ("40+ clients"). 2 = one specific result for a company like theirs.
4. Ask (0-2): 0 = asks for time (a call, minutes, a calendar link) or no question. 1 = vague or two asks. 2 = one small question they can answer in one word, or a free useful offer.
5. Short (0-1): 1 = 75 words or less from "Hi" to the sign-off.

Red flags (cap the score at 2): no subject line, ALL CAPS or hype words like FREE, "!!!" or "Act now", a follow-up that adds nothing new, a fake "Re:" or "Fwd:", any claim that can't be backed up.

Give me:
1) A table with each check, its score and one sentence on why.
2) The total, the word count, and any red flags.
3) A rewrite that scores 10/10: 4 parts only (their name and business fact, the problem, one proof, a one-word ask), under 75 words, plus a 2-4 word subject line.

Rules for the rewrite: use only facts I give you. Do not invent results, numbers or details. If a fact or proof is missing, write [ADD REAL PROOF] or [ADD FACT ABOUT THEIR BUSINESS] instead. No buzzwords, no exclamation marks, write like a person.

What I know about the prospect: [paste website text, news, job posts or a post they wrote]
My best real result: [one result for a company like theirs]
My email:
[paste your email]
Check the AI's math: AI tools often miscount words and add up scores wrong. Count the words yourself (Google Docs: Tools > Word count) and add the 5 numbers before you trust the total.

Your pre-send checklist

  1. Score it on the 5 checks. Send only at 9 or 10.
  2. No red flags: a real subject, no caps or hype, nothing made up.
  3. The first line is a fact about their business, not a compliment.
  4. One proof, for a company like theirs.
  5. One small question at the end. No meeting length, no calendar link.
  6. 75 words or less.
Bottom line: Polite isn't the same as good. Their name, their business, one proof, one small ask. That's a 10.

Quick answers

Is "I hope this finds you well" really that bad?

It's not rude. It's wasted. It takes the spot in the inbox preview where a fact about their business could go, and it tells the reader the email is a pitch. Keep "Hi Sarah," and go straight to them.

What score should I send at?

9 or 10. A 7 or 8 usually has one weak check, most often the ask or the proof, and fixing it takes a minute.

Can I ever ask for a meeting in a cold email?

Not in the first email. The 30MPC and Gong report found meeting asks cut replies by 44%. Ask for interest first. When they say yes, then book the call.

Do follow-ups use the same scorecard?

Yes, but the bar for length is lower: 1 to 2 sentences. Each follow-up must add something new, or it's a red flag and can't score above 2.
