The First-Call Pricing Script: Talk Price on Call One Without Scaring Buyers

This is the full first-call pricing script from the reel. Most sellers wait until the second or third call to talk about money. This guide shows why talking price on call one is linked to more wins, when in the call to do it, the exact words to use and how to answer whatever the buyer says next.

Why price belongs on the first call

Gong Labs looked at 11,331 sales opportunities, each with at least three calls, and compared win rates by when price first came up:

When price was first discussedWin rate
First call42%
Second call32%
Third call15%
Never5%

Budget showed the same pattern. When budget was discussed on the first call, the win rate was 49%, versus 7% when budget never came up. Gong also found it didn't matter who raised price first, the buyer or the seller.

This is a link, not proof that price talk causes the win. But it shows something useful: buyers who are serious want to talk about money early. Avoiding it just hides which deals are real.

When in the call to bring it up

Don't open with price. Gong suggests getting to pricing about three-quarters of the way through the first call, after you understand the problem and have shown how you'd help. In a 30-minute call, that's around minute 22. In a 45-minute call, around minute 34.

  1. First half: their problem, how they handle it now, what a good result looks like.
  2. Third quarter: how you'd solve it, with one short example.
  3. Around the 3/4 mark: give a price range, then ask about budget.
  4. Last few minutes: agree the next step and who else should join.

The script

Read it a few times, then say it in your own words. The brackets are where your details go.

1. The bridge

"Before we run out of time, I want to be upfront about cost, so you can tell me if we're even in the right range."

Why: it shows respect for their time. It also makes price a normal part of the call, not a scary moment at the end.

2. The range

"For a [company like yours / project like this], it usually lands between [$X] and [$Y] [a month / for the project]. Where you land depends mostly on [the 1-2 things that change the price]."

Why: a range feels like information, not a quote. Naming what moves the price lets them place themselves in it.

3. The budget question

"How does that compare with what you planned to spend on this?"

4. Listen, then answer what they actually said

The reel's last step is the one most people skip: actually listen to the answer. Repeat back what you heard before you reply: "So you'd planned closer to [$Z], and [what matters most to them] is the main thing. Did I get that right?"

Cut scope, not price: If the budget is lower, offer less work for less money, or a smaller first step. Dropping the price for the same work teaches them your first number wasn't real.

Worked example

Example math only, using Gong's rates as if they applied to you. Say you run 20 first calls a month:

  1. Price on call one: 20 × 42% = about 8.4 wins.
  2. Price only on call two: 20 × 32% = about 6.4 wins.
  3. Price never discussed: 20 × 5% = 1 win.

Your real numbers will be different. But even a small shift toward the first-call pattern adds up over a year, and you stop spending second and third calls on people who were never going to pay.

Set your range before the call

  1. Look at your last 10 closed deals. Write down the price of each.
  2. Drop the highest and the lowest. The rest is your honest range.
  3. Write down the 1-2 things that moved price up or down (users, scope, timeline).
  4. Practice saying the range out loud until it sounds calm.

Quick answers

Won't talking price early scare buyers off?

It may scare off people who could never pay, which saves you time. In Gong's data, deals where price came up on the first call won far more often than deals where it came up later or never.

What if my price really depends on the project?

Give the range most of your real projects land in, and name what moves it. "Most projects like this land between $X and $Y" is honest even when every project is different.

Should I wait for the buyer to ask?

No. Gong found it didn't matter who raised price first. If they don't bring it up, you do, around three-quarters of the way through the call.

What if they won't tell me their budget?

That's fine. Ask if your range is roughly where it needs to be. A yes, no or "depends" tells you most of what you need.
