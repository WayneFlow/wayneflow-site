Why boring rules beat clever tricks

Most people who say cold email is dead are breaking one of four basic rules. They send from a free Gmail address, they send the same email to everyone, they send one email and give up, or they answer replies a day later. None of these feel like big mistakes. Together, they cut your results by more than half.

The numbers in this guide come from Hunter's State of Email Outreach 2026, which looked at 31 million emails sent by Hunter users in 2025. Each rule below has its own number from that report, so you can see what it's worth.

Rule 1: Send from your own domain

Why it works: a free address (like name@gmail.com) looks like a stranger, not a business. You also can't set up your own sending records (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) on a free address. Hunter found emails from a custom domain got 5.2% replies vs 2.5% from free addresses. That's about twice the replies for the same work.

  1. Buy 1-3 extra domains that look like your brand, for example getacme.com or tryacme.com. Don't send cold email from your main domain, so your normal email stays safe.
  2. Create 2-3 inboxes per domain with real names, like sarah@getacme.com.
  3. Set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC for each domain. Your email provider has a step-by-step page for this (links in the resources below).
  4. Warm up new inboxes for about 2-3 weeks before you start cold sending, and keep each inbox to a small number of cold emails a day.
  5. Point each extra domain to your real website, so anyone who checks it lands somewhere real.

Quick check: Send one email from your new inbox to a Gmail and an Outlook address you own. If it lands in spam, fix your SPF, DKIM and DMARC before you send anything else.

Rule 2: Two real details per person, about their business

Why it works: a detail shows you looked at them, not at a list. Hunter found emails with two custom details got 5.6% replies vs 3.6% for emails with none. Hunter's survey also found that 67% of decision makers say personalization using public information makes them more likely to reply.

The key word is business. "I saw you like golf" is about the person. "I saw you opened a second location in Austin" is about their business, and it gives you a reason to write.

  • A new location, new product or new service on their website
  • A job post (hiring 3 sales reps means they want more sales)
  • A recent review, award or news story
  • What their website says they sell, and who they sell it to
  • The tool they use (shown on their site or in a job post)
Rule 2 email: two details, one question
Subject: {{detail_1_short}}

Hi {{first_name}},

Saw that {{company}} {{detail_1}}. Congrats.

Usually when a {{type_of_business}} {{detail_2}}, the next problem is {{problem}}. We fixed that for a {{similar_business}} and they got {{one_real_result}}.

{{your_name}}
AI prompt: find the two details for you
Here is a company website and any notes I have: [PASTE WEBSITE TEXT OR URL + NOTES]

Find 2 true, specific facts about this company's business (not about the person). Good examples: a new location, a new service, a job post, a recent review, who they sell to.

For each fact, write one short line I can use in a cold email, under 15 words, in plain English.

Only use facts you can see in what I gave you. If you can't find 2, say so. Do not guess.

Rule 3: Send three emails, not one

Why it works: people are busy. Your first email often arrives at the wrong moment. Hunter found that 3 emails got 6.8% replies vs 3.3% for 1 email, about twice the replies. Hunter also found the best sequences use three messages in total. Most replies still come from the first email, so make that one strong, and make each follow-up add something new.

Keep follow-ups in the same thread: Send follow-ups as replies to your first email, so they can see the whole story in one place. Keep each one shorter than the email before it.

Rule 4: Answer every reply fast, with one specific time

Why it works: when someone replies, they're thinking about your offer right now. An hour later they're in a meeting. A day later they've forgotten. ColdIQ, an outbound agency, wrote that after they cut their reply time to under 15 minutes, meeting booking rates from positive replies went up by over 30% (ColdIQ).

The second half of the rule matters just as much: offer one specific time. "When works for you?" starts a back-and-forth that can take days. "Does Tuesday at 2pm Eastern work?" can be answered with one word.

  1. Turn on phone notifications for replies in your sending tool, or forward replies to one inbox you check often.
  2. Answer every reply within your working hours, ideally within 5-15 minutes.
  3. Answer the question they asked first, in one or two lines.
  4. Offer one specific day and time, with the time zone. Add your booking link as a backup, not as the main ask.
  5. If they don't answer in 24 hours, send one short nudge in the same thread.

Your weekly checklist

  • All cold email goes out from your own extra domains, never a free address
  • Every email has two true details about the person's business
  • Every new lead is in a 3-email sequence
  • Replies are answered within 15 minutes during working hours
  • Every reply that's interested gets one specific time to meet
  • You track replies, interested replies and booked calls each week
Bottom line: Cold email isn't dead. Lazy cold email is. Four boring rules, done every day, beat any clever trick.

Quick answers

Can I send cold email from my main company domain?

It's safer not to. Use 1-3 extra domains that look like your brand, so if something goes wrong, your normal business email isn't hurt.

Do I really need to personalize every email?

Two true business details is enough. Hunter found that lifted replies from 3.6% to 5.6%. An AI prompt (above) can find them in under a minute per company.

Isn't a 3-email sequence annoying?

Not if each email is short, adds something new and the last one gives an easy way out. Stop after the third email if they don't reply.

What if I can't answer replies within 15 minutes?

Set phone alerts and keep ready-made replies (like the ones above) so each answer takes a minute. Even answering within the hour beats answering the next day.
