Why "sent by mistake" works

Your buyer scans their inbox in a second or two. Their brain sorts every line into two piles: people I know and companies selling to me. Marketing emails have long, polished, Title Case subject lines with numbers and hype. Coworkers write things like "quick question" or "budget".

A short lowercase subject line lands in the first pile. It looks like a forwarded note, a reply or something a colleague typed fast. That's the "sent by mistake" feeling, and it's what earns the open.

The honest part: Lowercase alone is not magic. In Belkins' study of 5.5 million emails, lowercase and Title Case did about the same. What changed results was length and tone: short, plain and not salesy. Lowercase is just the easiest way to make a line look human.

So the target is clear: 1-3 plain words, lowercase, about them, no pitch. The reel says 1-3 words. Belkins' sweet spot is 2-4. Treat 2-3 words as home base, and one word only when it's very specific (their company name, a project, a city).

The 5 rules

  1. 1-3 words. Phones cut subject lines after about 35 to 50 characters (Belkins). Short lines show in full and look like a quick note.
  2. All lowercase. No capital at the start, no Title Case. People typing fast to a colleague don't format. Keep real names normal if lowercase looks odd ("Acme" is fine).
  3. Boring on purpose. Pick a word a coworker would use: "hiring", "next quarter", "your site". Boring looks internal. Clever looks like marketing.
  4. About them, not you. Their company, their team, their project. Never your product name, never your offer.
  5. Match the first line. The subject line opens the door. The first line has to explain it right away, or the open turns into a delete.

How to test it (without fooling yourself)

Judge subject lines by replies, not opens. Open tracking adds a hidden image to your email, and Hunter's 2026 report found campaigns without open tracking got 7.4% replies vs 4.4% with it. Many inboxes also load images on their own, so open numbers are unreliable anyway.

  1. Write two subject lines for the same email: your current one (A) and a lowercase one (B). Change nothing else.
  2. Split one list in two random halves of at least 200 people each.
  3. Turn open tracking off. Send both on the same days, from the same inboxes.
  4. Wait until the sequence finishes, then compare reply rates.
  5. Keep the winner. Next round, test the winner against a new lowercase line.

Example: A goes to 200 people and gets 6 replies, a 3% reply rate. B goes to the other 200 and gets 12 replies, a 6% reply rate. B doubled the replies, so B becomes your new default. If the gap is only 1 or 2 replies, it's noise: test again on a bigger list before you decide.

Quick answers

Won't lowercase look lazy or unprofessional?

Not in a subject line. It reads as a quick note, which is the goal. Keep the email body clean, with normal spelling and punctuation.

Should the follow-ups use the same subject line?

Most tools send follow-ups as replies in the same thread, so they keep the first subject line. That's fine. You don't need a new one for each step.

Can I use their first name as the whole subject line?

Yes, it's one of the most human-looking options. Just make sure the first line gives a clear reason for writing.

How long should I test before picking a winner?

Until each version has gone to at least 200 people and the sequence has finished. Small gaps of 1 or 2 replies are noise.
