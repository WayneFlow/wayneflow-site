The short answer

Ask a stranger to repeat what you do in one sentence. If they can say it back, your offer works. If they can't, rewrite it. That's the whole test. It takes 10 seconds and it catches the most common reason good cold emails get ignored.

Your email can reach the inbox and still fail. If the reader can't tell in a few seconds who it's for and what they get, they won't reply. They won't ask you to explain either. They just move on.

Why a clear offer gets more replies

Readers decide fast. Instantly's 2026 benchmark report found the top senders, with reply rates 2 to 4 times the average, write first emails under 80 words with one clear call to action and lead with the reader's problem. There's no room in 80 words for an offer that takes three sentences to explain.

Unclear reads as irrelevant or salesy. In Hunter's State of Email Outreach 2026, 65% of decision makers said cold emails fail because they feel too sales-focused, and 61% said they fail because they aren't relevant. A vague offer full of big words hits both: the reader can't see that it's for them, and it sounds like a pitch.

One clear ask wins. Instantly says multiple calls to action dilute focus, and top performers use simple questions like "Worth a quick call?" Hunter found decision makers prefer soft asks like "Open to learning more?" over booking a meeting upfront. A clear offer makes a small ask easy to say yes to.

The 10-second stranger test

  1. Write your offer as one sentence. No commas doing the work of three sentences.
  2. Find someone who doesn't know your business: a friend, a family member, a person in another industry.
  3. Read the sentence to them once. Don't explain it.
  4. Ask: "Can you tell me in your own words what I do and who it's for?"
  5. Pass: they name the right buyer and the right result. Fail: they guess, hesitate, or ask "so what do you actually do?"
  6. If it fails, rewrite with the formula below and test again with a new person (the first one now knows the answer).
No stranger nearby?: Use the prompt at the end of this page. It's not as good as a real person, but it catches jargon and missing parts fast.

The setup checklist

The four steps from the reel. Each has the why, then exactly how.

Step 1: Say who it's for

Why: a reader only replies if they see themselves in the email. "Businesses" is nobody. "Roofing companies in Texas" is someone.

  • Name the buyer by industry or type: dental clinics, B2B SaaS founders, IT agencies.
  • Add one detail that narrows it: size, location or situation ("with 5 to 20 staff", "that just raised a seed round").
  • Use the words your buyer uses for themselves, not the words you use about them.

Step 2: Say the one result they get

Why: buyers pay for results, not for your process. If you list five results, the reader remembers none.

  • Pick the one result your best clients care about most.
  • Make it something they can see or count: more booked calls, fewer no-shows, faster invoices.
  • Only add a number if you can prove it. A made-up number destroys trust the moment they check.

Step 3: Use plain words a stranger would repeat

Step 4: Cut everything else

Why: every extra word is one more thing the reader has to skip to find the point. Keep the offer, cut the rest.

  • Cut your company history, awards and founding story from the first email.
  • Cut the full service list. Pick the one thing this list of buyers needs.
  • Cut "I hope this finds you well" and other filler before the offer (our opener swap sheet has replacements).
  • Keep one ask at the end.

The one-sentence offer formula

Fill in the blanks
We help [who it's for] get [one result] without [the pain they hate].

The last part is optional. Use it only if it's short and real. If your sentence is longer than about 20 words, you're explaining, not offering.

Common mistakes

  • Testing on a colleague. They already know what you do. Use a real stranger.
  • Explaining after you read it. If you have to add "what I mean is...", it failed.
  • One offer for every buyer. Different lists need different "who" and "result" lines.
  • Clever over clear. A smart-sounding line nobody can repeat loses to a plain one everybody can.
  • Adding numbers you can't prove. Clear and true beats big and made up.

Quick answers

Who counts as a stranger for the test?

Anyone who doesn't know your business and isn't in your industry. A friend, a neighbor or a family member works. Don't use a teammate or a client; they already know the answer.

Should my offer include a number?

Only if you can prove it with a real result. A clear offer without a number beats a big number the reader doesn't believe.

Can I have different offers for different buyers?

Yes, and you should. Write one sentence per list. A dental clinic and a SaaS founder want different results, so they need different "who" and "result" lines.

My offer passes, but nobody replies. Now what?

Then the offer probably isn't the problem. Check that you're emailing the right people and that your emails reach the inbox. Our deliverability checklist covers the checks.
