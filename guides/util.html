The numbers behind the carousel

SPI Research's 2026 Professional Services Maturity Benchmark, published with Deltek, found billable utilization hit 66.4%, its lowest point in nearly two decades. In 2021 it was 73.2%.

  • Top firms bill more. High-performance firms billed 75.0% of their time. Everyone else billed 64.9%.
  • Profit is thin. Industry EBITDA was 9.9% in 2025, well below SPI's expected 15% benchmark.
  • 75% is the target. Deltek's summary calls 75% the threshold SPI considers optimal. The survey covered over 500 firms.

Utilization means: billable hours ÷ total available hours. If a person works 40 hours and bills 26, their utilization is 65%.

Why this is a money problem, not a time problem

Every hour you don't bill still costs you the same salary, rent and tools. Going from 66.4% to 75% means billing about 3.4 more hours per person per week at a 40-hour week. You don't need more hours. You need more of the same hours to be paid for.

Worked example (the carousel math)

  1. Capacity: 5 people × 40 hours × 48 weeks = 9,600 hours a year.
  2. At 66.4%: 9,600 × 0.664 = 6,374.4, about 6,374 billable hours.
  3. At 75%: 9,600 × 0.75 = 7,200 billable hours.
  4. Gap: 7,200 − 6,374 = 826 hours (825.6 before rounding).
  5. Money: 826 × $100 = $82,600 a year (the sheet shows $82,560 because it doesn't round the hours).
  6. Per person: 825.6 ÷ 5 ÷ 48 = about 3.4 extra billable hours a week.

Step 1: Count real billable hours

A 40-hour week is never 40 billable hours. Email, meetings, sales and admin take time. A common rule is to plan on 75%, so 40 hours is really about 30 billable. Use 30, not 40, for planning and pricing.

You can't fix what you guess. For 2 weeks, everyone logs every hour into one of these buckets. A free time tracker or a shared sheet works.

BucketBillable?Examples

SalesNoProposals, discovery calls

Add up the client work hours, divide by 2 to get a weekly number, then divide by the number of people. That's your value for cell B5.

Step 3: Cut the non-billable

Look at the biggest non-billable bucket first. Example: 5 one-hour internal meetings a week is 5 hours a week per person. Turning 3 of them into a written update gives back 3 hours, almost the whole 3.4-hour gap in the example.

Problem you findFix
Too many internal meetingsReplace status meetings with a written update
Unbilled extras and free revisionsA clear scope with priced extras (see our SCOPE guide)

Not enough client work to fill hoursA pipeline problem: more sales conversations, not more effort
Senior people doing adminHand admin to a cheaper role or a tool

Step 4: Price on the hours you bill

If you set your rate by dividing cost by 40 hours, you are under-pricing. Divide by the billable hours instead.

Price on 40 hoursPrice on 30 billable hours
Weekly cost per person$1,500$1,500
Break-even hourly rate$1,500 ÷ 40 = $37.50$1,500 ÷ 30 = $50.00
Rate for a 50% margin (break-even ÷ 0.5)$75$100

Pricing on 40 hours sets break-even 25% too low ($37.50 vs $50). Every hour you can't bill eats your profit.

Quick answers

Is 75% right for every business?

It's the level SPI Research considers optimal for professional services firms. Very senior people and owners often bill less because they sell and manage. Set a target per role if you need to.

Should I count sales calls as billable?

No. They're important, but no client pays for them. Track them in their own bucket so you can see how much time sales really takes.

What if we're already at 75%?

Then focus on price: make sure your rate is set on billable hours, not 40. Going much higher than 75% often leads to burnout.

Why does the sheet show $82,560 and the carousel $82,600?

The carousel rounds the hour gap to 826 hours before multiplying by $100. The sheet uses the exact 825.6 hours.
