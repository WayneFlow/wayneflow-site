Day 1 vs day 30

On day 1, most people buy a domain, set up an inbox and send 100 cold emails. It feels fast. Then replies stop, because the emails are landing in spam. On day 30 of a slow start, the inboxes look calm and normal to Gmail and Outlook, and real replies come in.

The difference is not the copy and not the list. It's the slow start. This guide gives you the full 30-day calendar: what to do each week, why each step works, and the numbers to check every Monday. If you want the volume math (how many inboxes for 500 or 1,000 emails a day), use our sending calculator. This page is the calendar view of the same plan.

Step 1: Extra domains (days 1–2)

Why it works: cold email always carries some risk. If you send it from your main domain and something goes wrong, your normal emails to clients, invoices and password resets can start going to spam too. With extra domains, one domain stays safe: your real business email.

  1. Buy domains that look like your brand. For acme.com: getacme.com, tryacme.com, acmehq.com.
  2. Use .com where you can.
  3. Point each new domain to your real website, so anyone who checks it lands somewhere real.
  4. Create 3 inboxes per domain, with real names (anna@getacme.com), a photo and a signature.
  5. Add SPF, DKIM and DMARC to every domain before it sends a single email.
  6. Buy 1 or 2 spare domains and warm them up too, so you can swap one in without starting over.

Authentication is not optional anymore. Yahoo's sender rules say every sender needs SPF or DKIM at minimum, and bulk senders need both plus a DMARC policy. Microsoft's Outlook rules require SPF, DKIM and DMARC for domains sending more than 5,000 emails a day to Outlook, Hotmail and Live addresses. Since May 5, 2025, mail that fails goes to Junk, and Microsoft says it may later be rejected.

Why 3 per domain: 3 inboxes × 30 emails = about 90 a day per domain. That looks like a small team, not a blast. And if one domain gets in trouble, you lose 3 inboxes, not everything.

Step 2: Warm up for 14 days (days 1–14)

Why it works: a brand-new inbox has no history. If it suddenly sends to strangers, it looks exactly like a spammer. Warm-up gives it history first. Your inboxes swap normal emails with other real inboxes in a warm-up network. Those emails get opened, replied to and moved out of spam. Gmail and Outlook learn you're a real sender.

Instantly's warm-up guide says an account should be warmed up for at least 2 weeks before campaigns, and new accounts should stick to 10 warm-up emails a day. It also says never to turn on "disable slow warmup" for new accounts.

  1. Connect every inbox to your sending tool (Instantly, Smartlead or similar) and turn warm-up on, on day 1.
  2. Keep the tool's slow warm-up setting. Don't speed it up.
  3. Send zero cold emails for 14 days. Not even "just a few".
  4. Use the 14 days well: verify your lead list, write your emails, and set up Google Postmaster Tools for each domain.
  5. On day 14, check the warm-up health score of every inbox before you move on.

How to read the health score

In Instantly, the health score is the share of warm-up emails that land in the inbox instead of spam, over the last 7 days. Instantly's own example: 70 warm-up emails, 3 in spam, gives about 95.7% (67 ÷ 70). Instantly says the score should be above 90% before you start. We use a stricter line: 95 or higher. Note that it only measures warm-up emails, not your real campaign, so it's an early warning, not a guarantee.

Why it works: providers watch for sudden changes. A jump from 0 to 100 cold emails looks like a hacked account or a spammer. Small steps give providers time to see that people open, reply and don't complain. Instantly's 2026 benchmark report says new domains should start slow and build up over 4 to 6 weeks before full volume. This calendar reaches 30 a day in week 5.

  1. Days 15–21: 10 cold emails per inbox per day.
  2. Days 22–28: 20 per inbox per day, only if Monday's check is green.
  3. Day 29 on: 30 per inbox per day, again only if Monday's check is green.
  4. If any check is red, stay at the current step for another week. Don't step up.

Why stop at 30? In Hunter's State of Cold Email report (31 million emails sent in 2025), inboxes sending 20 to 49 emails a day got a 5.7% reply rate, against a 4.5% average. 30 sits safely in that range. Need more volume? Add inboxes. Never push one inbox to 100.

Send steady, not in bursts: Send about the same amount every working day. Instantly's benchmark report says teams that keep domain health stable and send consistently see 15 to 20% more replies, and that erratic volume kills deliverability.

Worked example: 2 domains, 6 inboxes

  1. Day 1: buy 2 domains, create 3 inboxes on each = 6 inboxes. Turn warm-up on.
  2. Days 1–14: 0 cold emails.
  3. Days 15–21 (5 working days): 6 × 10 = 60 a day, so 300 emails that week.
  4. Days 22–28 (5 working days): 6 × 20 = 120 a day, so 600 emails that week.
  5. Days 29–30 (Mon and Tue): 6 × 30 = 180 a day, so 360 emails.
  6. Total by day 30: 300 + 600 + 360 = 1,260 cold emails, from inboxes that are still healthy.
  7. From then on: 180 a day × about 22 working days = about 3,960 a month, and each domain sends only 90 a day.

Compare that with the day-1 rush: 6 inboxes × 100 = 600 emails on the first day. It looks faster. But if those emails land in spam, the domains are burned, and you start over with new domains and another 2 weeks of warm-up.

Step 4: Check every Monday

Why it works: domains rarely die overnight. The numbers slip a little each week first. A 10-minute Monday check catches the slip while it's still easy to fix.

Where the lines come from: Instantly's benchmark report says keep bounces below 2%. Hunter's report found the average bounce rate is 3.6%, which is why you verify every list first. Google's sender FAQ says keep the spam rate below 0.1% and never let it reach 0.3%. Yahoo also asks bulk senders to stay below 0.3%. 0.1% means 1 spam report per 1,000 emails.

Quick bounce math: At 180 emails a day, 2% is 3.6 bounces. So 4 or more bounces in one day from 6 inboxes means stop and check the list.

Never turn warm-up off

Warm-up is not a step you finish. It keeps a steady stream of good activity on each inbox, even after cold sending starts. Instantly's warm-up FAQ recommends keeping warm-up on for as long as you use the accounts. And don't switch warm-up tools or reset warm-up for no reason: the history you built is the point.

Common mistakes

  • Sending cold email from your main domain.
  • Skipping warm-up because "the domain is new, so it's clean". New is exactly the problem.
  • Going from 0 to 100 per inbox on day 1.
  • Turning warm-up off once campaigns start.
  • Stepping up the ramp when Monday's check is red.
  • Using a list nobody verified.

Quick answers

Can I skip warm-up if I buy pre-warmed inboxes?

Instantly says inboxes bought pre-warmed from them can be used in campaigns from day 1. Still keep warm-up on, ramp in steps, and run the Monday check. Pre-warmed doesn't mean you can send 100 a day.

Is 14 days always enough?

It's the minimum. Instantly suggests 2 to 4 weeks for new domains. If health scores are under 95 on day 14, keep warming up for another week.

What if a Monday check is red halfway through?

Don't step up. Stay at the current volume, or go back one step, keep warm-up on and fix the cause first (usually the list). Step up again only after a green Monday.

How do I get to more than 30 a day per inbox?

Don't push one inbox harder. Add more domains and inboxes and run them through the same 30-day calendar. Our sending calculator shows how many you need.
