Why it's four small mistakes

Most beginner cold emails don't fail because of one terrible line. They fail because of four habits that each cost a few replies: a lazy subject, a big ask, too many words and empty follow-ups. Fix all four and the gains add up.

Swap 1: the subject line

In Hunter's State of Cold Email report, based on 31 million emails sent by Hunter users in 2025, subject lines with "quick question" got a 2.5% reply rate and 28.7% open rate, vs 2.9% and 32.9% for other subject lines. Hunter adds that this depends a lot on your industry, so treat it as a sign, not a law.

What to use instead: test the person's first name ("Sarah") against a short subject about their world ("new reps at Acme"). Hunter's data found 5 to 6 words was the sweet spot for subject lines.

Swap 2: the calendar link

A calendar link says "go find 30 minutes for me". That's a lot to ask from someone who has never heard of you. It also adds a link to your first email, and many senders keep the first email plain for deliverability. This is a common rule, not a study.

What to use instead: offer one time. "Thursday at 10am your time?" can be answered with one word. If the time doesn't work, they'll usually suggest another one, and that's still a reply.

Worth a quick chat Thursday at 10am your time?

Swap 3: the 400-word email

Short is a common rule for a reason: people read cold emails on their phone, between other things. Hunter's data is more mixed than most advice: emails of 61 to 80 words got relatively high reply rates, but so did emails of 181 to 200 words. Under 75 words is a safe starting point, not a magic number.

What to use instead: four short parts. A reason you're writing to them. The problem. One line of proof. One ask.

Before (the starter pack)

Subject: quick question. "Hi Sarah, I hope this email finds you well. My name is John and I'm the founder of XYZ Agency. We are a full-service growth agency that has helped companies across many industries..." (and 350 more words, then a calendar link)

After (under 75 words)

Rewritten email
Subject: Sarah

Hi [first name],

Saw [company] is hiring two new sales reps. Congrats on the growth.

New reps usually need meetings on day one, not month three. We helped [similar company] book [real result] in [time].

Worth a quick chat Thursday at 10am your time?

[your name]

Fill the brackets with real details. Never invent a result. If you have no case study yet, offer something useful instead, like a short teardown of how they get leads today.

Swap 4: "just bumping this"

Follow-ups matter a lot. In the same Hunter report, using three messages instead of one increased total replies by 106% (6.8% vs 3.3%). But Hunter also found that people who followed up three or more times had a lower average reply rate. So: send about 3 emails, and make each one count.

"Just bumping this" gives the reader nothing new to reply to. Every follow-up should add one new thing: a new angle, a case study, or a soft close.

The 3-email sequence

Email 2: a new angle (3 days later)
Hi [first name],

One more thought. Most [their role]s we talk to don't have a lead problem, they have a follow-up problem: [the pain in one line].

If that sounds familiar, happy to share how [similar company] fixed it. Worth a look?

[your name]
Email 2 option: a case study
Hi [first name],

Quick example: [similar company] had the same [problem]. We [what you did, one line]. Result: [real number] in [time].

Want me to send the details?

[your name]
Email 3: the soft close (4 to 5 days later)
Hi [first name],

I'll stop here so I don't crowd your inbox.

If [problem] becomes a priority later, just reply "later" and I'll check back next quarter.

[your name]

Worked example: what follow-ups add

Using Hunter's averages on a list of 1,000 people:

  1. One email only: 1,000 × 3.3% = about 33 replies.
  2. Three emails: 1,000 × 6.8% = about 68 replies.
  3. Same list, same offer: about 35 more replies from two good follow-ups.

These are averages from Hunter's users. Your numbers depend on your list and your offer.

Quick answers

Is "quick question" always bad?

No. In Hunter's data it did a bit worse on average, and Hunter says it depends on your industry. Test it against a first-name subject on your own list.

When can I use a calendar link?

After they say yes. Once someone replies "sure, let's talk", a calendar link saves both of you time.

What if my offer needs more than 75 words to explain?

The first email only needs to start a conversation, not explain everything. Save the details for when they reply.

How many days between emails?

A common rule is 2 to 4 days between emails. The bigger point is that each one adds something new.
