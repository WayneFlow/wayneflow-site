The short answer

David Ogilvy ran one of the most famous ad agencies in the world. He died in 1999, but his main rule about headlines fits cold email better than almost any advice written this year. Most people read the headline and stop. In cold email, the headline is your subject line. If the subject line doesn't earn the open, the body, the offer and the call to action never get read.

So a good subject line follows four rules: short, about them, plain, and honest about what the email gives. Then you don't guess which one works. You write 10, keep the best 2 and test them.

Where the rule comes from

In Confessions of an Advertising Man (1963), Ogilvy wrote that on average:

Five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy.

His point was simple. If most readers only see the headline, the headline has to do most of the selling. Print ads have changed. People haven't. Your prospect scans an inbox full of names and subject lines and decides in a second what to open. Same reader, new inbox.

What this means for you: Spend real time on the subject line. Many people write the email for an hour and the subject line in ten seconds. Ogilvy would flip that.

Rule 1: Short (1 to 3 words)

Why it works. A short subject line shows in full on a phone and looks like a quick note from a real person, not a campaign. Belkins studied 5.5 million cold emails sent in 2024 and found subject lines of 2 to 4 words got the highest open rate (46%). Lines over 9 words dropped to about 35%. They also note phones cut subject lines off after about 35 to 50 characters.

  1. Write your subject line the way you normally would.
  2. Cut every word that doesn't carry meaning: "quick", "just", "regarding", "opportunity", "for you".
  3. Stop when you have 1 to 3 words. If it still makes sense, it's done.
  4. Check it on your phone. If any of it is cut off, it's too long.
Don't overthink the exact number: Hunter's 2026 report (31 million emails) found 5 to 6 words did best for reply rate, while Belkins found 2 to 4 words did best for opens. Both agree on the main point: short beats long. Start at 1 to 3 words and test from there.

Rule 2: About them, not you

Why it works. People notice their own name, their company and their own problems first. A subject line about your product looks like an ad. A subject line about them looks like it might matter. In the Belkins study, personalized subject lines got a 46% open rate vs 35% without personalization. Hunter found subject lines with two personal details (for example, first name and company) got a 40.2% open rate vs 35.4% with just one.

  1. Pick one thing that belongs to them: their first name, their company, their city, a job they're hiring for, a recent launch or post.
  2. Put that thing in the subject line. Leave your company and product out.
  3. If you can, add a second detail: "[first name] / [company]" or "[company] hiring".
  4. Make sure the first line of the email explains why you used it.

Rule 3: No ad. Plain words, no selling

Why it works. The subject line's only job is to get the email opened. Hype words, urgency and discounts make it look like marketing, and people skip marketing. Belkins found that marketing hype, urgency words like "now" or "ASAP" and generic greetings pulled open rates below 36% (source).

  1. Read the line out loud and ask: would a coworker send me this?
  2. Remove words like "free", "exclusive", "limited", "guaranteed", "act now" and any price or percent off.
  3. Remove exclamation marks, emojis and ALL CAPS words.
  4. Never fake a reply or forward with "Re:" or "Fwd:". It gets the open and loses the trust.

Rule 4: Promise what the email gives

Why it works. Ogilvy's headlines worked because the ad delivered what the headline promised. In cold email, the subject line and the first line of the body are one unit. If the subject says "your hiring plans" and the email opens with a pitch about software, the reader feels tricked and deletes it, or marks it as spam. A matching subject line makes the first sentence feel like the answer to a question they already had.

The better way: write 10, keep the best 2, test

Why it works. Ogilvy didn't trust one headline. He wrote many and tested them. Your first idea is rarely your best one, and your opinion is not data. Writing 10 forces you past the obvious lines. Testing 2 tells you which one real buyers answer.

  1. Write 10 subject lines for the same email, using the four rules. Mix types: name, company, trigger, problem.
  2. Cut to the best 2. Drop any line that is over 4 words, about you, salesy, or doesn't match the first line.
  3. Split one list in two random halves, at least 200 people each. Same email body, same inboxes, same days. Only the subject line changes.
  4. Turn open tracking off and judge on replies. Hunter found campaigns without open tracking got a 7.4% reply rate vs 4.4% with it (source), and open counts are unreliable anyway.
  5. Wait until the sequence finishes, then compare reply rates. Keep the winner.
  6. Next round, test the winner against a new line from your list of 10. Repeat every few weeks.

Prospect: [first name], [job title] at [company].
What I know about them: [hiring, launch, new location, recent post, problem].

Your checklist before you hit send

  • Short: 1 to 3 words, fully visible on a phone.
  • About them: their name, company, trigger or problem. Not your product.
  • Plain: no hype, no urgency, no caps, no fake "Re:".
  • Honest: the first line of the email delivers what the subject line promises.
  • Tested: you wrote 10, kept 2, and are measuring replies, not opens.

Quick answers

Did Ogilvy really say five times more people read the headline?

Yes. He wrote it in Confessions of an Advertising Man (1963), about print ads. The exact ratio was about newspapers and magazines, but the idea carries over: far more people see your subject line than read your email.

Should I put my company name in the subject line?

Usually no. Your name already shows in the sender field. Use the space for something about them, like their company or a trigger.

Do follow-ups need new subject lines?

No. Most tools send follow-ups as replies in the same thread, so they keep the first subject line. That's fine and keeps the conversation together.

How many people do I need for a fair A/B test?

At least 200 per version, and wait until the full sequence ends. If the winner is ahead by only 1 or 2 replies, treat it as a tie and test again.
