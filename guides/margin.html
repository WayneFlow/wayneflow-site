Why margins matter more than more clients

BenchPress 2025, reported by Productive.io, found that in 2024, average gross profit margins for £1M+ agencies fell to 39%, the lowest on record. Their target: at least 50%.

A low margin isn't always a pricing problem. Often it's one or two clients who take far more time than their fee covers. More clients like that just make you busier. The first step is to see each client's margin on its own.

The one formula

Margin = (fee − delivery cost) ÷ fee.

Delivery cost is everything it takes to do the work for that client: people hours × cost per hour + tools + contractors. If a client pays $8,000 and costs $5,600 to serve, the margin is ($8,000 − $5,600) ÷ $8,000 = 30%.

Count your own hours: The carousel's rule: it won't work unless you count your own hours as delivery cost. Owners who skip their own time always think their margins are better than they are.

How to work out cost per hour

Cost per hour is what one hour of a person's time costs the business, not what you charge. A simple way:

  1. Take what the business pays the person per month (salary, taxes and benefits).
  2. Divide by the hours they actually work in a month.
  3. For yourself, use what you'd have to pay someone to do your job.
  4. Use one blended number for the whole team if that's simpler. The example below uses $80 an hour.

Paste it into Google Sheets

  1. Open a new Google Sheet.
  2. Click the Copy button on the box below.
  3. Click cell A1 and paste. The tabs split it into columns.
  4. Select column H and click Format > Number > Percent.
  5. Replace the example clients with yours. To add a client, copy row 4 down and move the TOTAL row below it.

Make the flag turn red

  1. Select cells A2 to K4 (all client rows).
  2. Click Format > Conditional formatting.
  3. Under "Format cells if", choose Custom formula is and enter: =$H2<0.5
  4. Pick a red fill and click Done. Any client under 50% now shows in red.

Client A is the problem. Two ways to get it to 50%:

  • Cut the cost to $4,000 (column K: $8,000 ÷ 2). That means about 20 fewer hours a month at $80, since $1,600 ÷ $80 = 20.
  • Raise the fee to $11,200 (column J: $5,600 ÷ 0.5). Check: ($11,200 − $5,600) ÷ $11,200 = 50%.

And the owner-hours trap: in Client A's 60 hours, say 10 are yours. Leave them out and delivery cost drops to $4,800, so the margin looks like 40%. The real number is 30%.

Reprice without losing the client

Use column J as your target, then give the client a choice: keep the scope at the new fee, or keep the fee with less scope.

Repricing email
Hi [first name],

Quick heads-up before we plan next quarter. The work for [company] has grown since we started: we now spend about [hours] hours a month on it, up from [old hours].

To keep the quality where it is, here are two options:
1) Keep everything as it is at [new fee] a month, from [date].
2) Keep the current fee and focus on [the 2-3 things that matter most], dropping [the rest].

Happy to talk it through. Which works better for you?

[your name]

Narrow what you sell

Doing fewer things, more often, usually makes delivery faster and cheaper. In the 2026 State of Digital Services report by Promethean Research (119 agencies, 2025 data), average net margin was 13%, while agencies that reduced their services grew the fastest and averaged 30% net margins. The picture isn't simple, though: Promethean also noted that service generalists beat service specialists on profit that year. So narrowing isn't magic. Use your sheet to see which service you deliver at the best margin, and sell more of that.

Promethean also found only 59% of agencies tracked individual project margins. Just using this sheet every month puts you ahead of many agencies.

Check your clients with Claude

Margin check prompt
Here are my clients for last month (one per line):
[client name, monthly fee, hours (team + mine), tools cost, contractor cost]
My cost per hour: $[number]

1) Work out delivery cost and margin for each client: (fee - delivery cost) / fee. Show the math.
2) Flag every client under 50%.
3) For each flagged client, show the fee needed for 50% (delivery cost / 0.5) and the most I can spend at today's fee (fee / 2).
4) Suggest one practical fix for each: cut hours, cut tools, change scope, or reprice.
5) Write a short, friendly repricing email for the worst one, offering two options: same scope at the new fee, or same fee with less scope.
Simple English, no jargon.

Quick answers

Is 50% gross margin realistic for a small agency?

It's the target BenchPress gives. Many agencies are below it, which is exactly why checking each client helps. Start by getting your worst client to 50%.

Gross margin or net margin, what's the difference?

This sheet shows gross margin: fee minus the cost of doing the work. Net margin also takes off rent, sales, admin and other overhead, so it's always lower.

What if a low-margin client is my biggest one?

Don't drop them overnight. Reprice at renewal, cut the hours that don't matter, and win new clients at healthier margins so you depend on them less.

How often should I check?

Every month. Hours drift up slowly, and a client at 55% can be at 40% six months later without anyone noticing.
