Why cutting your fee hurts more than it looks

In Promethean Research's agency study of 151 agency leaders (2024 data), fee cuts were linked to a 6% drop in revenue. Agencies that raised their rates grew faster and had above-average margins. That's a link, not proof that one causes the other. But it matches what the math below shows.

The reason is simple. A discount comes only out of your profit. Your costs stay the same. Your people still work the same hours.

The discount math

Example: a project with a $10,000 fee that costs you $6,000 to deliver (people, tools, contractors). Your profit is $4,000.

  1. Give 10% off: the fee is now $9,000. Your cost is still $6,000.
  2. Your profit drops from $4,000 to $3,000. That's a 25% cut in profit from a "10%" discount.
  3. To make $4,000 again, you need $4,000 ÷ $3,000 = 1.33 times the work. That's 33% more work for the same money.

The shortcut: Extra work needed = discount ÷ (your margin − discount). At a 40% margin, 10% off means 10 ÷ 30 = 33% more work. If your margin is low, even small discounts get very expensive.

Build it in a spreadsheet

Put the fee in A2, your delivery cost in B2 and the discount in C2 (as a percent, like 10%). Then add these formulas:

Discount Calculator formulas
A1: Fee                    A2: 10000
B1: Delivery cost          B2: 6000
C1: Discount               C2: 10%

D1: Profit now             D2: =A2-B2
E1: Margin now             E2: =D2/A2
F1: New fee                F2: =PRODUCT(A2,1-C2)
G1: New profit             G2: =F2-B2
H1: Profit you lose        H2: =(D2-G2)/D2
I1: Extra work needed      I2: =D2/G2-1

Format E, H and I as percentages.
If G2 is zero or negative, the discount wipes out all your profit.

This assumes extra clients cost the same to deliver as this one. Count your own hours in delivery cost, or the numbers will look better than they are.

Option 1: cut scope, not price

If the client really has less budget, give them less work, not the same work for less money. A common rule: remove one deliverable instead of cutting 10%.

Option 2: trade, don't give

If you lower the price, get something back. A common rule: a lower price only in exchange for something that has real value to you. Here are the 5 replies.

Reply 1: paid upfront
I can do [lower price] if the full amount is paid upfront before we start. That saves us the admin and the risk, so we can pass it on. If you'd rather pay monthly, it stays at [normal price]. Which works better for you?
Reply 2: a longer term
That price works on a 12-month agreement. Longer terms let us plan our team, so we can price it lower. On a month-to-month plan, it's [normal price]. Want me to send both options?
Reply 3: a case study
Here's an idea: I can do [lower price] if you're open to a short case study once we hit results. Just your name, the numbers and a quote. If you'd rather keep it private, totally fine, it's [normal price].
Reply 4: less scope
I want to make this work for your budget. At [their budget], I'd suggest we drop [one deliverable] and keep [the core work]. You still get [main result]. We can add [the deliverable] back later. Sound good?
Reply 5: a smaller first step
How about we start smaller? A [pilot length] pilot with [smaller scope] for [pilot price]. If it works, we move to the full plan at [normal price]. You see results before the full commitment.

Option 3: a smaller first step

A pilot is not a discount. It's a smaller job at a fair price. The client lowers their risk, you keep your rate, and if it works, the full fee follows. Write the full price and the next step into the pilot proposal so nobody is surprised.

The part that makes it work: be ready to walk away

All of this only works if you can say "no problem, maybe later" and mean it. If this deal is the only one in your pipeline, you'll cave. Keep enough deals in progress that losing one isn't scary.

Quick answers

Is it ever OK to give a discount?

Yes, when you get something real back: cash upfront, a longer term, a case study or less work. A discount for nothing is the one to avoid.

What if they say a competitor is cheaper?

Ask what's included in the cheaper quote. Often it's less scope. Then offer Reply 4: match their budget by removing a deliverable, not by cutting your rate.

Won't I lose deals by holding my price?

Some, yes. But each discount deal costs more work for less profit. The calculator shows how much. Losing a bad-margin deal can be a win.

How do I know my delivery cost?

Add up the people hours (including yours) at a real hourly rate, plus tools and contractors for that client. Do it once per client and you'll see who's really profitable.
