The short answer

In the campaign in the reel, one client sent 84,643 emails in one real month and got 2,399 replies. About 1 in 28 people replied. That sounds great until you flip it: if you only email 28 people, you should expect one reply. Maybe zero.

So when a small campaign gets no replies, the reply rate usually isn't the problem. Your crowd is too small. The fix is simple math: decide how many replies you want, then work out how many right-fit people you need to reach to get them.

Why 28 people tell you nothing

Each person has a small chance of replying. With small groups, luck decides the result, not your email. Even if your campaign truly gets 1 reply per 28 people, here's what chance alone does:

People emailedReplies you'd expect (1 in 28)Chance of getting zero replies

Read the first row again. More than 1 time in 3, a campaign that works will show zero replies from 28 people. Most people quit right there and rewrite a good email. With a few hundred people, zero replies almost never happens by luck. If you see it at that size, something is really wrong, and now you know it.

The math behind it: Chance of zero replies = (27 ÷ 28) to the power of the number of people. For 28 people: (27 ÷ 28)^28 ≈ 0.36, so about 36%.

Big crowd, small rooms

Wait, isn't smaller better? Hunter found that sequences sent to 21 to 50 people got a 6.2% reply rate, vs 2.4% for 500 or more. But read Hunter's own takeaway: they are not telling you to stop at 50. They say to cut your audience into smaller segments and write copy that fits each one, then scale that.

So you need both. A big total crowd, so you have enough people to get real replies and real data. And small rooms inside it, so every email feels like it was written for that reader. A crowd of 5,000 is not one giant blast. It's 100 rooms of 50.

The outreach checklist

Six steps. Do them in order. Each one has the why and the how.

1. Write down who fits (and who doesn't)

Why: a big list of the wrong people is still the wrong people. Hunter's survey found 61% of decision makers say cold emails fail because they aren't relevant. Fit rules keep your crowd big *and* right.

  • Industry: the 1 to 3 industries where your best customers come from.
  • Company size: a range of employees or revenue, not "any".
  • Location: the states, regions or countries you can actually serve.
  • Role: the job titles of the person who says yes (the buyer, not the user).
  • A sign they need you now: hiring, new location, new funding, bad reviews, running ads, and so on.
  • The no list: current clients, past clients who left, competitors, partners and anyone who asked you to stop.

2. Count your whole crowd

Why: the total number of right-fit people tells you two things before you send a single email: whether your goal is possible, and how many months you can run before you email the same people again.

  1. Put your fit rules into your lead database, LinkedIn search or Google Maps search. Write down the number of companies that match.
  2. Pick how many people you'll contact per company. Hunter found that emailing 1 or 2 people per company got a 5.1% reply rate, vs 3.5% for 3 or more. Start with 1 or 2.
  3. Total crowd = companies × people per company.
  4. Expect to lose some to verification (step 5). Write down the crowd you can really reach.

3. Work out how many people you need

Why: "send more emails" is not a plan. A number is. Working backwards from replies tells you exactly how big your crowd has to be each month.

  1. People to reach = replies you want × people per reply. (At 1 in 28: 40 replies × 28 = 1,120 people)
  2. Raw contacts to pull = people to reach ÷ the share that passes verification. (1,120 ÷ 0.8 = 1,400)
  3. Companies to find = raw contacts ÷ people per company. (1,400 ÷ 2 = 700 companies)
  4. Months of runway = reachable crowd ÷ people to reach per month.
Which reply rate to use: If you have your own numbers from at least a few hundred people, use them. If not, start with Hunter's 3% for sales and replace it after your first full month.

4. Split the crowd into small rooms

Why: Hunter's data says tight segments win, and Instantly's 2026 benchmark report names micro-segmentation as one of the biggest reasons top senders reach 10%+ reply rates while the average is 3.43%.

  • Split by one thing at a time: industry first, then role or company size, then location or signal.
  • Give each segment its own first line and its own proof (a result from their world).
  • Aim for sending groups of about 50 people who have a lot in common. You can run many of them.
  • Name every segment clearly (for example: "Dental - owners - Texas - hiring") so you can compare results later.

5. Verify before you send

Why: a crowd you can't reach doesn't count. Hunter found the average bounce rate is 3.6%, and Instantly says to keep bounces under 2%, because every bounce hurts your sender reputation. Google's sender guidelines also tell senders to stop emailing addresses that bounce again and again.

  • Run every list through an email verifier before it goes into your sending tool.
  • Remove invalid emails. Be careful with catch-all addresses: Instantly warns they carry a higher bounce risk.
  • Remove duplicates and everyone on your no list.
  • Write down how many contacts survived. That's your real keep rate for the math in step 3.

6. Let the whole sequence finish, then judge

Why: a person isn't done until they get every email in the sequence. Instantly found 58% of replies come from the first email and 42% from follow-ups, and Hunter found 3 emails got 6.8% vs 3.3% for 1 email. Judge too early and you miss almost half your replies.

  • Judge an angle (offer, first line, proof) only after a few hundred people finished the full sequence. Add up all the small rooms that used it.
  • Compare segments to each other, not to your hopes.
  • Keep what works, rewrite what doesn't, and send it to the next few hundred.

Worked example

An IT services company wants 40 replies a month from office managers and owners at small firms. These are example numbers to show the math, so swap in your own.

StepMathResult
People to reach40 replies × 281,120 people a month
Raw contacts (80% pass verification)1,120 ÷ 0.81,400 contacts
Companies (2 people each)1,400 ÷ 2700 companies a month
Whole market6,000 companies × 2 × 0.89,600 reachable people
Runway9,600 ÷ 1,120about 8.6 months

Now the rooms. The 6,000 companies split into 3 industries × 2 size bands = 6 segments of about 1,000 companies. Each month the company pulls about 117 companies from each segment (700 ÷ 6 ≈ 117) and sends them in groups of about 50 with copy written for that group.

Compare that with the room of 28. Email 28 people and you expect 1 reply, with a 36% chance of none. Email 1,120 people with the same email and you expect about 40, and a bad week can't fool you.

If your runway is short: Under 3 months of runway means your fit rules are too tight for the goal. Add a nearby industry, a second buyer role or a new region, and size it again before you send.

Common mistakes

  • Judging a campaign on 20 to 50 people. Luck runs the result at that size.
  • Fixing the small crowd with a bad list. More of the wrong people only adds noise and spam complaints.
  • One giant blast. A big crowd needs many small rooms, each with its own copy.
  • Emailing 4 people at every company. Hunter's data favors 1 or 2.
  • Skipping verification to save time. Bounces burn the inboxes you need for the whole crowd.
  • Calling it after email 1. About 4 in 10 replies come from follow-ups.

Crowd size prompt
Act as my cold email list planner.

What I sell: [one sentence]
My best customers: [industry, company size, location, buyer job title]
Replies I want per month: [number]
My reply rate per person: [use 3% if I don't know]
People I'll contact per company: [1 or 2]
Share of contacts that pass verification: [use 80% if I don't know]
Companies that fit, if I know it: [number or "unknown"]

Do this:
1) Write my fit rules and a no list in a short table.
2) Calculate people to reach, raw contacts and companies needed per month. Round up.
3) If I gave a company count, calculate my runway in months. If it is under 3 months, suggest 3 ways to widen the crowd without losing fit.
4) Split the crowd into 4 to 8 segments and give each one a name, a likely problem and one first-line idea.
5) Tell me how many people must finish the sequence before I judge each angle.
Show everything in tables. No hype.

Quick answers

Isn't "1 in 28" just a reply rate?

It is, but counted per person, not per email. Many sending tools count replies per email sent, and a sequence sends several emails to each person. When you size your crowd, plan in people, because people are what you run out of.

What if my whole market is only a few hundred companies?

Then go deep, not wide. Make every email very personal, add a second buyer role at each company only if the first one doesn't answer, and use other channels too. Hunter found 50.5% of decision makers now prefer LinkedIn for outreach.

How big should each segment be?

Send in groups of about 50 people who share an industry, role and problem, like Hunter's best-performing range. But judge each angle on the total across its groups, a few hundred people, not on one group of 50.

Can I just buy a bigger list?

Only if it fits your rules and passes verification. A bigger list of the wrong people adds bounces and "not interested" replies, not sales. Count right-fit people, not rows in a spreadsheet.
