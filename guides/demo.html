Why using your product beats describing it

Most cold emails describe the product: the features, the platform, the team. The reader has seen that line a hundred times, so they skim it. A feature list is about you. A result is about them.

This email does the opposite. You run your product (or a one-minute version of it) on the prospect's own company first. Then you put their result in the email: "3 found." Not a feature. Not a case study about someone else. Their own result, from their own website or ads. That's news to them, and people read news.

The data backs this up. In Hunter's State of Email Outreach 2026, 61% of decision makers said cold emails fail because they aren't relevant, 48% called out generic, impersonal messages, and 65% said they fail because they feel too sales-focused. On the other side, 67% said personalization using public information makes them more likely to reply. Even light personalization helps: emails with two custom details averaged a 5.6% reply rate vs 3.6% for emails with none.

Instantly's 2026 benchmark report found the average reply rate is 3.43%, while the top 10% of campaigns get 10.7% or more. What the top senders do: problem-first messages ("lead with the problem, not your solution"), first emails under 80 words, and one simple call to action. A result-first email is all three at once.

The approachWhat the line saysWhat the reader thinks
Features"Our platform scans 200+ SEO factors with AI-powered insights."Zzz. So does everyone.

Proof beats claims: A claim asks the reader to trust you. Their own result doesn't need trust. They can check it in 10 seconds. Show, don't tell.

The 5 steps

01. Pick one quick result

Why: you need a result you can produce for every lead, fast, without a meeting. If it takes an hour, you'll do it for 5 leads and stop. If it takes about 1 minute (or less, once it's automated), you can do it for your whole list.

A good quick result is a small slice of what you actually sell. It should be countable ("3 found"), public (you got it from their site, ads or listings, not anything private), tied to money or customers, and something you can fix. Here are examples across industries:

02. Run it on THEIR company

Why: the whole email rests on one fact. It has to be about their company, today, and it has to be right.

  1. Use their real domain. Take it from their email address (dana@harbordental.com → harbordental.com), not from a guess based on the company name.
  2. Run the check on the page that matters most: the homepage, the main service page, or their live ads.
  3. Save the raw result for every lead: the count, one specific example (a URL, an ad, a review) and the date you ran it.
  4. If the check finds nothing, don't fake it. That lead gets a different email (see the FAQ).

03. Put the result IN the email

Why: the result is the hook. It goes in the subject line and the first line, with one specific example so they know it's real. Everything about you is cut down to one short line, or left out.

Result-first email template
Subject: {{count}} {{issue}} on {{domain}}

Hi {{first_name}},

I ran {{domain}} through {{your tool or check}} this morning. {{count}} found. One of them: {{one specific example}}.

{{One line on what it costs them, in plain words.}}

Want the full list with the fix for each? It's free. I'll just send it over.

{{your name}}

Line by line: the subject tells them there's a result about them (Hunter found 5 to 6 words is the sweet spot for subject lines). The first line shows you actually did the work. The example proves it's real. The cost line says why it matters. The ask is small: "want it?", not "book 30 minutes". Hunter also found decision makers prefer open asks like "Can I send more info?" over booking a meeting up front.

04. Offer the full version free

Why: the email showed a slice. The free offer gives them a reason to reply right now, and the reply is what starts the conversation. Make the free thing something you can deliver fast, with no call needed.

  • SEO tool or agency: the full list of broken pages, with where each one should redirect.
  • Ads agency: a short teardown of their 3 active ads, with one rewrite.
  • Web or speed: the top 3 fixes from the speed report, in plain English.
  • Software with a free trial: the full report, already run on their site, waiting in an account.
  • Reviews tool: drafted replies to their unanswered reviews, ready to paste.
Free, but useful: Don't offer "a free consultation". That's a meeting, not a gift. Offer the full version of the thing you just showed them.

Why: doing this by hand works for 10 leads. For hundreds a week, you need the check and the first draft to run on their own, so your time goes into checking facts, not copying and pasting.

Clay is a common tool for this. You put your leads in a Clay table, one row per lead, and add columns that run for every row. Two column types matter here:

  • HTTP API column (Clay docs): calls any API for each row and puts the answer in your table. Use it to run your own product's API, or a public one like the PageSpeed Insights API, with the lead's domain inserted from the row. You pick which fields from the response to keep, and you can set a rate limit.
  • Use AI column (Clay docs): has a Web research (Claygent) option that reads and analyzes websites, and a Create or modify content option that writes text from the data already in your row. Use web research to find the result when there's no API, and content to write the email line.
  1. Import your leads into a Clay table with first name, company and domain.
  2. Add an HTTP API column (or a Use AI → Web research column) that runs your quick check on the domain. Save the count and one example as separate fields.
  3. Add a run condition so the email step only runs when the count is 1 or more. Clay's docs call these conditional runs. They also stop you wasting credits on empty rows.
  4. Add a Use AI → Create or modify content column with the prompt at the bottom of this guide. Feed it only the check's output, not the whole website.
  5. Test on one row first, as Clay's docs recommend. Then 10 rows. Read every one.
  6. Run the quality check below, then send the approved rows to your sending tool.
No Clay?: Any setup that runs a check per row works: a spreadsheet with a script, Make or Zapier, or your own product's bulk export. For a small list, just do it by hand. The tool doesn't matter. The right result in the email does.

The quality check: one wrong fact kills it

This tactic won't work unless the result is right. If you say they have 3 broken pages and they have 0, you're not just ignored. You look careless, and the reader trusts nothing else you say. A wrong fact is worse than no fact.

Hunter's data supports a human pass: emails that were manually edited before sending averaged a 5.2% reply rate vs 4.4% for fully automated ones. Hunter also found 69% of decision makers are bothered when AI was used to write an email, unless it feels genuinely human. Checking each line by hand helps with both.

  1. Right company: the domain in the result matches the domain in their email address.
  2. Right count: the number in the email matches the raw check output exactly.
  3. Real example: click the one example (the URL, the ad). It shows what the email says it shows.
  4. Fresh: the check ran close to the send date. Sites change. Re-run anything that's more than a few days old.
  5. Fair: the line describes the problem, it doesn't insult them. "3 pages show an error" is fine. "Your site is a mess" is not.
  6. No guesses: nothing in the email that isn't in the check output. No made-up revenue loss, no "you're losing customers every day".

Common mistakes

  • Describing the check instead of showing the result. "Our tool scans your whole site" is still a feature. "3 found" is a result.
  • Using a result that takes an hour. You won't do it for every lead. Pick a one-minute slice.
  • Leaving out the example. "We found some issues" sounds like spam. One specific URL, ad or review proves it's real.
  • Overselling the problem. Don't turn 3 broken pages into "you're losing thousands of patients". Say what you found and stop.
  • Sending without checking. One wrong fact kills the email, and it can hurt your name with the rest of their team.
  • Putting the full report in the first email. Give a slice, offer the rest. The reply is the point.

The copy-paste prompt

Paste this into Claude (or a Clay Use AI column) for each lead. It turns the lead's data and the raw check output into a result-first email, and it refuses to write one when the data isn't good enough.

Result-first email prompt
You write short, result-first cold emails. You never describe the product. You show the prospect a result from their own company.

What I sell: [one sentence]
The free thing I offer: [e.g. the full list of broken pages with a fix for each]

Lead data:
- First name: [first name]
- Company: [company]
- Domain: [domain]

Raw check output (the ONLY facts you may use):
[paste the result: count, examples, date run]

Rules:
1. Use only facts from the raw check output. Never guess, round up or add numbers.
2. If the output is empty, shows 0 found, is unclear, or the domain in the output doesn't match the lead's domain, write only: SKIP, and the reason.
3. Subject line: 4 to 6 words, with the count and the domain, e.g. "3 broken pages on [domain]".
4. First line: say you ran [domain] through the check, then the count ("3 found.").
5. Give exactly one specific example from the output (a URL, an ad, a review).
6. One plain line on why it matters to them. No made-up costs or revenue.
7. End by offering the free thing, as a small question. No meeting request, no calendar link.
8. Under 75 words in the body. Plain text. No buzzwords, no exclamation marks. Write like a person, not a marketer.

Return:
SUBJECT:
BODY:
FACT USED: (copy the exact fact from the output)
CHECK: (what a human should click to confirm it)
Bottom line: Don't tell them what your product does. Do it to their company, put the result in the email, and offer the rest for free. Then make sure the result is right.

Quick answers

What if my product can't produce a result in a minute?

Pick the first step of what you do and run only that. A full audit takes days, but one check (broken pages, a speed score, their live ads) takes a minute. The email shows the slice. The free offer and the call cover the rest.

Won't prospects find this creepy?

Not if you use public information. In Hunter's 2026 survey, 67% of decision makers said personalization using publicly available information makes them more likely to reply. Stick to their website, public ads and public reviews.

What if the check finds nothing?

Don't send the result-first email, and never invent a problem. Send that lead your normal email, or run a different check. A clean result can also be a short, honest compliment, but don't expect it to pull as many replies.

Do I need Clay to do this?

No. Clay makes it easy to run a check and write a draft for every row, but a spreadsheet, a script or doing it by hand for a small list all work. What matters is that every result is right before it sends.
