The result behind the reel

The Kiln, a Clay-focused agency, built four to five million dollars in new pipeline for a single client with this play, according to Smartlead's case study. They took signals from the client's competitors, built a list of lookalike companies, and wrote a personal line for each person in Clay before sending through Smartlead.

Why this works

  • They already buy this kind of product. You don't have to explain the problem. They know it.
  • You have an easy reason to write. "Companies like yours use [type of tool]" is a natural opener.
  • Most senders ignore them. Everyone else emails random lists. Your competitors' customers, and companies just like them, get fewer relevant emails than you think.

The 5 steps

Step 1: Pick the rivals

Write down 3 competitors your buyers compare you with. Use the names your prospects mention on calls, not the biggest names in your market.

Step 2: Find their customers, then build a lookalike list

Why: a competitor's public customers show you exactly what a buyer looks like. Then you find more companies just like them.

Where to lookWhat you find
Competitor's website: case studies, logos, testimonialsNamed customers
Review sites like G2Reviewers' company size and industry
Tech lookup tools like BuiltWith (for web tools)Sites that use a competitor's product
Job posts that mention the competitor's productCompanies that use it today
  1. Collect 10 to 20 named customers from the sources above.
  2. Write down what they share: industry, size, country, how they sell.
  3. Use those filters in your lead tool to find companies that look the same (the reel's example: 8 known customers turned into 16 lookalikes, a small, tight list).
  4. Cut anything that doesn't fit, even if the list gets smaller.
Stay fair: Never trash the competitor, and never claim to know private details about their contract. Talk about the result you give, not about what's wrong with them.

Step 3: Signals first

Why: a company that is changing is more likely to buy now. Email those first. In the reel, 3 of the companies on the list were "hot".

  • New funding round
  • Hiring for the team that uses your product
  • New leader in that department
  • Complaints about the competitor in public reviews
  • Contract renewal season (if you know when they signed)

Step 4: One real detail per person

Why: one true line shows you looked at their company. The Kiln used Clay to pull a unique data point for each person and turn it into the first line. Use Clay's AI research columns or do it by hand for small lists.

First-line prompt (for Clay or ChatGPT)
Company: [company name]
Website: [url]
Recent news or signal: [funding / hiring / new leader / review]

Write one first line for a cold email to the [job title] at this company. Use only the facts above. Under 20 words. Plain, friendly English. No compliments, no "I hope this finds you well". If there's no real fact to use, reply NONE.

Step 5: Slow warm-up, weekly fixes

Warm up new inboxes for about 14 days before sending, start slow, and check your numbers every week. Pause anything with bounces over 2% or falling replies.

Copy-paste email template

Competitor play email
Subject: {{company}} + [your company]

Hi {{first_name}},

{{first_line}}

We help [type of company] like [similar customer] get [result] without [pain the competitor's customers often have].

[Your name]

Quick answers

Is it legal to email my competitor's customers?

Contacting a business about your product is normal B2B outreach. Follow the cold email rules for your country, include an easy opt-out, and don't make claims about the competitor.

What if my competitors don't list their customers?

Use review sites, job posts and tech lookup tools. Or skip straight to lookalikes of your own best customers.

How big should the list be?

Small and tight beats big and loose. Start with a few hundred well-matched people, test, then grow.
