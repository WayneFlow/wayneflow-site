Why the 30-minute call ask fails

A stranger doesn't know you yet. Asking for 30 minutes of their week is a big ask before they've seen anything useful. Most people say nothing, because saying no feels rude and saying yes feels like a lot.

A small ask flips that. "Worth a look?" can be answered with one word. One word is easy, and a reply starts a conversation.

The Ultimate Cold Email Data Report from 30 Minutes to President's Club and Gong, based on 85M+ cold emails, puts it simply: make an offer instead of asking for a meeting. In its chart, offer-style asks scored about 28% more replies, and meeting asks about 44% fewer.

The idea: Ask for a word, not a calendar slot. The calendar comes after the yes.

The 12 asks to copy

Give something free

  1. "Can I send the playbook we used for a company like yours?"
  2. "Want me to send over the 1-page audit of [their website / their outreach]?"
  3. "I put together a list of [10 companies like their ideal client]. Want it?"
  4. "Happy to send the template we use for this. Should I?"

Show the proof

  1. "Happy to show you the exact campaign we ran for [similar company]. Open to it?"
  2. "Want to see the numbers from a [their industry] client? I can send a screenshot."
  3. "Worth a look at how [similar company] did it?"

Take the risk off

  1. "If it doesn't [specific result] in [time], you don't pay. Worth a look?"
  2. "Can I run a small test for you first, before you decide anything?"
  3. "No contract to start. Want me to send how the first month works?"

Check interest

  1. "Is [their problem] on your list this quarter?"
  2. "Worth a look?"

What makes a good "something free"

The free thing has to be worth one word. "A free consultation" usually isn't, because it's still a call. A good free thing is specific, real and about them.

WeakStrong
A free consultationThe exact campaign we ran for a firm like yours
Our company deckA 1-page audit of your current outreach
A demoA 2-minute video showing your website with 3 fixes
"More info"The list of 10 companies we'd target first for you

After the yes: send it, then offer one time

This is where most people go wrong. They get "sure" and reply with a calendar link and a pitch. Keep your promise first.

  1. Send what you offered, the same day. No call needed to get it.
  2. Add one line on why it's relevant to them.
  3. Offer one time to walk them through it, not a calendar link: "Want me to walk you through it Thursday at 10?"
  4. If they don't answer, follow up once in a few days with a new angle or a question about what they sent you.
After-the-yes reply
Hi [first name],

Here it is: [link or attachment].

The part most relevant to you is [section], because [one reason tied to their company].

Want me to walk you through it Thursday at 10? Takes 15 minutes.

[your name]

Quick answers

Isn't asking for a call the whole point?

Yes, but not in the first email. Get a small yes first, deliver something useful, then offer one time for a call. People who've already said yes once are more likely to say yes again.

What if I don't have anything free to send?

Use an interest ask like "Is [their problem] on your list this quarter?" Then build one simple asset, like a 1-page checklist or a case study, as soon as you can.

Which of the 12 asks works best?

It depends on your buyer. Pick 2 and test them on similar groups of people. Keep the one with more positive replies.

Should I still follow up if they don't reply?

Yes. Each follow-up should add something new, like a different free thing or a short proof point, not "just bumping this".
