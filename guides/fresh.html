Why the big database stops working

Big lead databases are useful, but everyone uses the same ones. When you filter for "marketing directors at 50 to 200 person software companies", thousands of other senders run that same filter. The people on that list get cold emails every day, and they've learned to ignore them.

The data also goes old. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reports the median worker had been with their employer 4.1 years (January 2026), so many people in any database have already moved on. Cold email operator Christian Plascencia says raw database pulls are only 50 to 60% a real fit by his count, and that 30 to 50% of contacts move or change details within six months (Cold Email Daily's summary). Those are his numbers, not a study, but they match what most senders see: old, shared data means bounces and silence.

What a "fresh" list really means

  • Fresh timing: the company did something recently that shows they need you (hiring, ads, funding, a new location).
  • Fresh people: you checked that the person is in the role today.
  • Fresh emails: every address was verified this week.
  • Fresh reason: your first line is about that recent event, not a guess.

A list like this is small. That's fine. Hunter's State of Email Outreach 2026 found that campaigns sent to 21 to 50 people got 6.2% replies vs 2.4% for 500+ people.

The 6 steps to build the list

1. Pick one buyer and one signal

Why: the signal is your reason to write. Without it, you're just another cold email. How: finish this sentence: "I help [job title] at [type of company] who just [signal]." Example: "I help founders of 10 to 50 person agencies who just posted a job for a sales rep."

2. Collect the companies

Search the source for your signal and copy each company into a sheet: name, website, the signal, and the link that proves it (the job post, the ad, the filing). Aim for 50 companies, not 5,000.

3. Find the right person

Why: the person feeling the problem is rarely the generic "info" contact. How: check the company's team page or LinkedIn for the buyer title. Hunter found that emailing 1 or 2 people per company got 5.1% replies vs 3.5% for 3 or more, so pick one, maybe two.

4. Find and verify the email

  1. Use an email finder (most have a small free plan) or the company's own website to get the address.
  2. Run every address through an email verifier before it goes into a campaign.
  3. Remove invalid ones. Put catch-all and risky addresses in a separate small batch.
  4. Keep your bounce rate under 2%. Hunter's average is 3.6%.

5. Write one first line from the signal

Why: the signal is proof you looked. Hunter found that emails with two custom details got 5.6% replies vs 3.6% for emails with none. How: one short sentence about the signal, then the problem that usually comes with it.

6. Send in small batches, then refill

Send each list of about 50 as its own campaign. Next week, go back to the same source and pull the new companies. A fresh list is a weekly habit, not a one-time export.

Prompt: turn a signal into a first line

Copy into ChatGPT or Claude
You write first lines for short B2B cold emails.

What I sell: [one sentence]
Who I'm writing to: [job title, company type]
The signal: [paste the job post title / ad / news headline / filing]

Write 3 first lines.
Rules: under 20 words, mention the signal in plain words, no flattery, no "I noticed" or "I came across", sounds like a real person typed it.
Then write one second line that names the problem this signal usually brings.

Quick answers

Do I have to stop using lead databases?

No. Use them to find people and emails, but start from a fresh signal. The signal decides who goes on the list.

How many leads can I build by hand in a week?

Start with one list of about 50. Once your steps are smooth, add more lists. Quality beats size here.

Which signal works best?

It depends on your offer. Hiring for the role you help with is a strong start for most B2B services. Test a few, one list each, and keep the winner.
