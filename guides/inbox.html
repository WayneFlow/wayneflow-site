Copy problem or deliverability problem?

If your best email and your follow-up both land in spam, nobody reads them. Rewriting the copy changes nothing, because the problem happens before anyone sees a single word. So before you touch the copy, find out which problem you have.

Don't use open rates to decide. Google's sender guidelines say Google doesn't track open rates and that low open rates aren't necessarily an accurate sign of a spam problem. Use the signs below instead.

0 replies of any kind. Not even out-of-office or "remove me" replies.DeliverabilitySend a test to your own Gmail and Outlook (Check 2 below)

"If you want to know whether you're actually inboxing, watch your out-of-office replies. When those drop, you're going to spam, usually 48 to 72 hours before it fully tanks." (Quoted in Hunter, State of Email Outreach 2026)

Hunter's report also found a 3.6% average bounce rate and warns that above 2% you're damaging your domain. A bad list is a deliverability problem too, so verify every list before it goes in.

The 4 checks on one card

Run these in order. If any check fails, don't send. One failed check can send every email to spam, no matter how good the copy is.

Check 1: Domain

Why it works: your domain is your name tag. A free address looks like anyone could have sent it, and you can't set up your own records on it. In Hunter's study of 31 million emails, emails from a custom domain got 5.2% replies vs 2.5% from free addresses. A separate sending domain also protects your main domain. If the sending domain gets a bad reputation, your client emails and invoices still arrive.

  1. Buy a domain close to your brand. For acme.com, try getacme.com or acmehq.com. Stick to .com where you can.
  2. Point the new domain to your real website, so anyone who types it in lands somewhere real.
  3. Create your inboxes with a real email provider (Google Workspace or Microsoft 365), using real names like jane@getacme.com.
  4. Never send cold email from your main domain. Keep that one for clients, invoices and normal mail.

Check 2: Records (SPF, DKIM, DMARC)

Why it works: these three DNS records prove the email really came from you. Google requires SPF or DKIM from every sender, and SPF, DKIM and DMARC from anyone sending more than 5,000 emails a day to Gmail. Google also recommends that you always set up all three, and says unauthenticated messages might be marked as spam or rejected. So treat it like the reel says: one missing record means guilty until proven innocent.

RecordPass testDetail that trips people up
SPFTest email shows spf=passOnly one SPF record per domain. It must list every service that sends for you.
DKIMTest email shows dkim=passGmail needs a key of at least 1024 bits. Google recommends 2048 bits if your provider supports it (Google DKIM help).
DMARCTest email shows dmarc=passLives at _dmarc.yourdomain.com. Start with p=none, as Google recommends, and add rua= so you get reports.
AlignmentThe From: domain matches the SPF or DKIM domainRequired by Gmail and Yahoo for DMARC to pass. Sending through a tool on a different domain breaks it.
Starter DMARC record (TXT)
Host / Name:  _dmarc
Type:         TXT
Value:        v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc@yourdomain.com

(Replace yourdomain.com with your sending domain. Make sure the dmarc@ inbox exists, or use any inbox you check.)
  1. Run each sending domain through Google's free Check MX tool. Fix anything it flags.
  2. Send a plain test email from each new inbox to a Gmail address you own.
  3. In Gmail, open the email, click the three dots, then Show original. Look for SPF: PASS, DKIM: PASS and DMARC: PASS.
  4. Do the same with an Outlook.com address. Microsoft explains how to view message headers. Look for spf=pass, dkim=pass and dmarc=pass under Authentication-Results.
  5. Any line that isn't PASS? Fix that record, wait for DNS to update, and test again. Exact values to paste are in our DNS records guide.
One test per inbox: Test every inbox, not just one per domain. A typo in one inbox's setup only shows up when that inbox sends.

Check 3: Warm-up (14 days, no shortcuts)

Why it works: a brand-new inbox has no history. If its first act is 50 emails to strangers, it looks exactly like a spammer. Warm-up sends and receives normal-looking emails with other inboxes so providers learn you're real. Instantly's warm-up guide says to wait at least 2 weeks before using accounts for campaigns, and Instantly's 2026 benchmark report says new domains need 4 to 6 weeks of gradual increase to reach full volume.

Check 4: Inboxes (many, not one)

Why it works: each inbox and each domain builds its own reputation, and Google tracks volume per domain and IP. Google Workspace allows up to 2,000 emails a day per user (Google's limits page), but that's a system limit, not a safe cold email number. In Hunter's data, accounts sending 20 to 49 emails a day got 5.7% replies vs 4.5% overall. So keep each inbox at about 30 cold emails a day and add inboxes to grow.

What your email provider handles: Forward and reverse DNS (PTR) and secure TLS connections are handled by Google Workspace and Microsoft 365. You mostly need to set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC yourself.

Worked example: 300 cold emails a day

  1. Inboxes: 300 ÷ 30 = 10 inboxes.
  2. Domains: 10 ÷ 3 = 3.3, so round up to 4 domains (3, 3, 2 and 2 inboxes).
  3. Records: 4 domains × 3 records (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) = 12 records to add, then 10 test emails to Gmail and 10 to Outlook, one from each inbox.
  4. Warm-up: days 1 to 14, zero cold emails. Use this time to build and verify your list.
  5. Ramp: days 15 to 21: 10 inboxes × 10 = 100 a day. Days 22 to 28: 10 × 20 = 200 a day. Day 29 onward: 10 × 30 = 300 a day.
  6. Spam rate check: Google measures spam rate on mail that reaches Gmail inboxes. If 2,000 of your emails reach Gmail inboxes in a month, 0.1% is just 2 spam reports and 0.3% is 6. That's why the list and an easy opt-out matter so much.

Copy-paste prompt: run the pre-send check

Pre-send deliverability check
Act as a cold email deliverability expert. Before I send a single email, check my setup against these 4 checks: domain, records, warm-up, inboxes.

My setup:
- Main business domain: [domain]
- Sending domains: [list]
- Email provider: [Google Workspace / Microsoft 365]
- Inboxes per domain: [number]
- For each domain, paste the SPF, DKIM and DMARC lines from a test email's headers (Gmail: Show original): [paste]
- Days of warm-up on each inbox so far: [number]
- Cold emails I plan to send per day in total: [number]
- Is my list verified? Bounce rate on last send: [yes/no, %]

For each check, say PASS or FAIL and why. For every FAIL, give the exact fix and tell me if I can send yet. Then tell me how many inboxes and domains I need at 30 cold emails per inbox per day and 3 inboxes per domain, rounding up. Be strict: if any check fails, the answer is "don't send yet".

The pre-send checklist

  • ☐ Sending from a separate domain, not my main domain and not @gmail.com
  • ☐ Every inbox shows SPF: PASS, DKIM: PASS and DMARC: PASS on a test email
  • ☐ DMARC record at _dmarc with p=none and a rua= address
  • ☐ Every inbox has at least 14 days of warm-up and warm-up stays on
  • ☐ Inboxes = daily goal ÷ 30, with no more than 3 inboxes per domain
  • ☐ List verified, so bounces stay under 2%
  • ☐ A plain opt-out line in every email, and opt-outs removed within 2 days
  • ☐ Google Postmaster Tools set up for each sending domain
  • ☐ Ramp plan written down: 10, then 20, then 30 a day per inbox
The rule: All boxes ticked? Send. One box empty? Fix it first. Skip these and your best email goes straight to spam.

Quick answers

I send fewer than 5,000 emails a day. Do I still need DMARC?

Gmail only requires SPF or DKIM below that number, but Google recommends always setting up SPF, DKIM and DMARC. It takes 5 minutes and removes one reason to land in spam. Set it up.

Can I skip warm-up if my domain is a few years old?

Don't send cold email from your main domain at all. A new inbox has no sending history even on an older domain, so give every new inbox at least 14 days of warm-up.

How do I know if I'm in spam right now?

Send a test from each inbox to your own Gmail and Outlook addresses, check Google Postmaster Tools, and watch your out-of-office replies. If those suddenly drop, pause and test before you send more.

Do cold emails need an unsubscribe link?

Google's and Yahoo's one-click rules are written for marketing and subscribed messages from big senders. For cold email, at minimum give a plain way to opt out, like "Not the right person? Just reply and I won't email again," and remove people within 2 days.
