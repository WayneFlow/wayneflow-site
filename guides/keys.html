Never Send Cold Email From Your Main Domain: The Safe Setup, Step by Step

This is the full guide from the reel. It shows why your main domain must stay out of cold email, how to set up separate sending domains that protect it, and how to ramp up slowly so your normal emails to clients keep landing in the inbox.

The short answer

Send cold email only from separate domains that you buy just for outreach. Keep your main domain for the emails that matter: clients, partners, invoices and your team. If a cold email domain gets a bad reputation, you replace it. If your main domain gets a bad reputation, you have a real business problem.

One rule: Your main domain is your home address. Cold email domains are rented rooms. Never test risky things at home.

Why your main domain is at risk

Inbox providers judge reputation at the domain level, not only the single address. Hunter's cold email guide says a separate email address under your main domain is not enough, because the reputation of the entire domain is what receiving servers analyze. Hunter also warns that sending cold email without precautions can quickly destroy that reputation.

Apollo's help center says the same thing in plain words: it takes very little time to burn a sending reputation, and rebuilding it is slow and difficult.

  • Your client emails go to spam. Proposals, invoices and replies start landing in the junk folder.
  • You may not notice for days. Nobody tells you your email went to spam. Clients just go quiet.
  • It is hard to undo. A bad reputation can take weeks to repair, and you still have to run your business meanwhile.
  • One bad list can cause it. Bounces and spam complaints from a single campaign are enough.

What a safe setup looks like

Suped's deliverability guide uses this same split: cold outreach gets its own separate domain, while employee mail stays on the root domain.

Step 1: Pick your sending domains

  1. Start from your brand name. If your site is acme.com, think of names close to it that still look real.
  2. Use a clean pattern. Lemlist lists patterns that work, such as adding a prefix or suffix with no separator (tryacme.com, acmehq.com) or a clean TLD swap (acme.io).
  3. Avoid weird names. No random letters, no long names, no hyphens or numbers.
  4. Buy more than one. Spread your sending over several domains so one problem never stops everything.
  5. Check the name is free at your registrar, then buy.

Step 2: Make each domain look real

Apollo's guide says filters do automated checks on a domain, including looking for a website, so a simple landing page is enough. Lemlist adds that a sending domain should point to your main website, because a domain that goes nowhere is a red flag for providers.

  • Point the domain to your main site with a simple redirect or a one page site.
  • Set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC on every domain. Follow our DNS records guide and the 10 minute domain fix.
  • Use a custom tracking link on the sending domain if your tool offers one, so links do not point back to your main domain.
  • Keep links in your emails on the sending domain, never your main domain.

Step 3: Set up inboxes and warm them up

  1. Create a few inboxes per domain. Keep the number small. Our sending calculator guide shows the math.
  2. Use real looking names. First name at the domain works well.
  3. Turn on warm-up in your sending tool for about 2 weeks before real campaigns.
  4. Check the inboxes yourself. Send a test to a Gmail and an Outlook address and see where it lands.

Step 4: Ramp slowly

A new domain has no history. Start with a small number of emails a day per inbox and increase a little each week. Do not jump from zero to a big number on day one. Slow growth looks like a normal business. A sudden spike looks like a spammer.

WeekWhat to do
1 to 2Warm-up only. No cold emails yet.
3Start with a small batch to your best verified leads.
4Increase a little if bounces and replies look healthy.
5 and afterReach your normal daily volume per inbox and hold it.

A worked example

Say your main domain is acme.com and you want to start cold email. You buy tryacme.com and acmehq.com, point both at acme.com, set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC, create 2 inboxes on each (4 in total) and warm them up for 2 weeks. If tryacme.com has a bad week and lands in spam, you pause it and keep sending from acmehq.com. Your clients keep reading your emails from acme.com the whole time.

Honest note: A separate domain is not a magic shield. Suped notes that modern filters can sometimes connect related domains, and a consistently poor cold email strategy can still hurt your brand. Good targeting and clean lists matter just as much as the setup.

The safe setup checklist

  • Main domain used only for real business email
  • 2 or more separate sending domains bought
  • Each domain redirects to your main website
  • SPF, DKIM and DMARC set on every domain
  • A few inboxes per domain, not many
  • 2 weeks of warm-up before any campaign
  • Slow ramp over the first 4 to 5 weeks
  • Lists verified before sending
  • Test emails checked in Gmail and Outlook

Quick answers

Is a subdomain enough instead of a new domain?

A fully separate domain gives the safest isolation. Subdomains can share reputation with the main domain, so for cold email a separate domain is the safer choice.

How many domains do I need?

Start with two or three so one problem never stops everything, and add more as your daily volume grows.

How long before I can send real campaigns?

Plan for about 2 weeks of warm-up, then a slow ramp over a few weeks.

Will a separate domain fix bad targeting?

No. It protects your main domain, but bad lists and bad copy will still burn the sending domain, so keep your lists clean.
