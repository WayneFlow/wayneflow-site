Why deals stall

Forrester's State of Business Buying 2024 found that 86% of B2B purchases stall during the buying process. It also found that, on average, 13 people inside a company are involved in the buying decision. You usually talk to one of them.

Those people don't always agree. In a Gartner survey of 632 B2B buyers, 74% of buying teams showed "unhealthy conflict" during the decision. Buying groups that reached agreement were 2.5 times more likely to report a high-quality deal.

So pushing your one contact harder doesn't help much. They're not the problem. The other 12 people are missing information, and your contact doesn't have an easy way to give it to them.

What a decision brief is

A decision brief is one page your contact can forward to everyone involved. It answers the questions those people will ask, in the order they'll ask them. It's not a proposal and not a sales deck. It's a short summary that makes the decision easy to discuss.

  • One page. If it's longer, nobody reads it and nobody forwards it.
  • Written for the group. Gartner found messages aimed at the whole buying group help them agree. Messages aimed at one person can do the opposite.
  • In their words. Use what your contact told you on the call, not your marketing copy.

The 5 sections

SectionThe question it answersLength
1. The problemWhat's wrong, and who does it affect?2-3 lines
2. The cost of waitingWhat does it cost us to do nothing for 3 more months?2-3 lines with a number
3. The optionsWhat can we do, including doing nothing?3 options, 1 line each
4. The price rangeRoughly what does it cost?1-2 lines
5. The next stepWhat happens next, who decides, and by when?1-2 lines
Always include "do nothing": Doing nothing is the option most buying teams pick. Name it, and show what it costs. Then it's a real choice, not a silent default.

The template

One-page decision brief (copy it)
DECISION BRIEF: [what's being decided, in one line]
Prepared for: [company] team | Date: [date]

1. THE PROBLEM
[2-3 lines in their words. Who it affects: e.g. sales team, finance, operations.]

2. THE COST OF WAITING
[The number: hours, money or deals lost per month.]
Waiting 3 more months costs about: [number].

3. THE OPTIONS
A) Do nothing: [what happens].
B) [Option B, e.g. do it in-house]: [cost, time, risk in one line].
C) [Option C, e.g. work with us]: [cost, time, risk in one line].

4. PRICE RANGE
[Option C] usually runs [$X to $Y] [per month / per project], depending on [what changes the price].

5. NEXT STEP
[One clear step, e.g. a 30-minute call with finance and ops.]
Who needs to agree: [roles].
Target decision date: [date].

How to work out the cost of waiting

This is the section that moves deals. Most teams don't stall because they think the problem is small. They stall because nobody has put a number on it. Use this simple math:

  1. Find the unit of pain: hours lost, deals missed, money wasted.
  2. Multiply by the people or volume it affects.
  3. Turn it into a monthly number, then a 3-month number.

Worked example

Say your contact runs a sales team of 5 reps. Each rep loses about 3 hours a week to manual data entry. Their time costs the company about $50 an hour. (These are example numbers. Use the ones your contact gives you.)

  1. 5 reps × 3 hours × $50 = $750 a week
  2. $750 × 52 weeks ÷ 12 months = $3,250 a month
  3. 3 more months of waiting = $9,750

Now the brief can say: "Waiting until next quarter costs about $9,750 in rep time." If your option costs less than that, the conversation changes from "maybe later" to "why wait?"

A filled-in example

Example brief
DECISION BRIEF: Automating CRM data entry for the sales team
Prepared for: Acme Supply team | Date: [date]

1. THE PROBLEM
Each of our 5 reps spends about 3 hours a week typing call notes and updates into the CRM. Affects sales and the sales manager's reports.

2. THE COST OF WAITING
About $3,250 a month in rep time (5 reps x 3 h x $50/h, every week).
Waiting 3 more months costs about $9,750.

3. THE OPTIONS
A) Do nothing: reps keep losing 15 hours a week combined. Reports stay late.
B) Build it in-house: needs IT time, about 2-3 months before it works.
C) Use [vendor]: live in about 2 weeks, team trained in one session.

4. PRICE RANGE
Option C usually runs $800 to $1,200 a month, depending on the number of users.

5. NEXT STEP
30-minute call with the sales manager and finance next week.
Who needs to agree: sales manager, finance lead, IT.
Target decision date: end of this month.

How to send it

Send it the same day as the call, while it's fresh. Make it easy to forward.

Email to send with the brief
Subject: One-page summary for your team

Hi [first name],

Thanks for the time today. You mentioned [names or roles] will want a say, so I put everything on one page you can forward as is: the problem, what waiting costs, the options and a price range.

If anything's off, tell me and I'll fix it before you send it around.

[your name]

Quick answers

Isn't a proposal the same thing?

No. A proposal is long and about your offer. The brief is one page and about their decision. Send the brief first, the proposal once the group agrees.

What if I don't know the cost of waiting?

Ask on the call: "Roughly how many hours or deals does this cost you a month?" If you still don't know, leave a blank for your contact to fill in. Never make it up.

Should I include competitors as options?

You can say "another vendor" as an option. Keep it fair. A brief that only makes you look good won't be trusted by the rest of the team.

What if my contact won't forward it?

Ask who else will be part of the decision, and offer a short call with them. If no one else ever appears, the deal may not be real yet.
