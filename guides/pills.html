The short answer

A month has about 4.33 weeks (52 weeks ÷ 12 months). So 29 calls a month ÷ 4.33 = 6.7, about 7 calls a week. Spread over the whole calendar, that's about 1 call a day (29 ÷ 30 days).

That's the real pace of the home services company in the reel: about 29 booked sales calls a month, every month, for 3 months, and $165,000 in sales, after they had relied on Facebook ads. It wasn't a miracle. It was a habit: a steady amount of work every single day.

Working days vs calendar days: The reel counts all 7 days, so 29 calls is about 1 a day. Most B2B calls happen Monday to Friday, so plan on working days: 4.33 weeks × 5 days = 21.65 working days, which makes 29 calls about 1.3 to 1.4 a working day.

Why a weekly number beats a monthly goal

  • You find problems in days, not months. If you only check "29 this month?" on the 30th, it's too late to fix anything. A weekly check gives you 4 chances a month to fix it.
  • It's small enough to feel doable. 29 feels big. 7 a week, or about 1 a day, feels like a normal Tuesday.
  • It forces steady sending. Calls come from replies, and replies come from emails sent every day. A weekly target stops the "big push at the end of the month" habit.
  • Steady sending gets more replies. Instantly's 2026 benchmark report found that teams that keep domain health stable and send consistently see 15 to 20% more replies, and it says erratic volume kills deliverability.

Step 1: Break your monthly goal into a weekly and daily number

Why: you can't act on a monthly number. You can act on today's number. How: do these three divisions for your own goal.

  1. Calls per week = monthly goal ÷ 4.33.
  2. Calls per working day = monthly goal ÷ 21.65 (or calls per week ÷ 5).
  3. Round the weekly number up and use it as your target. Rounding up gives you a small buffer for no-shows and short weeks.

Step 2: Find your emails per booked call

Why: calls don't come from nowhere. Each booked call costs a certain number of cold emails. Once you know that number, your daily call target turns into a daily sending target you control.

How: multiply your three funnel rates together, then divide 1 by the result. Use your own numbers if you have 2 to 4 weeks of data. If you don't yet, start with careful guesses like these:

RateExample starting numberWhere it comes from
Reply rate (replies ÷ emails)3%A little under the 3.43% average in Instantly's 2026 report. Hunter's 2026 report puts the average sequence at 4.5%.
Interested rate (interested ÷ replies)24%Our full-quarter campaign: 1,061 interested out of 4,372 replies
Booked rate (calls ÷ interested)22%Our 5-day blitz: 25 calls from 115 interested

3% × 24% × 22% = 0.001584. Then 1 ÷ 0.001584 = about 631 emails per booked call. That's an example, not a promise. Swap in your own rates as soon as you have them.

Step 3: Work backwards to daily sending volume

Why: this is the number you can do something about every morning. How: multiply calls by emails per call, then divide down to a working day.

Worked example: 29 calls a month

  1. Goal: 29 booked calls a month.
  2. Per week: 29 ÷ 4.33 = 6.7, so aim for 7 calls a week.
  3. Emails per month: 29 × 631 = 18,299 emails.
  4. Emails per week: 18,299 ÷ 4.33 = about 4,226.
  5. Emails per working day: 4,226 ÷ 5 = about 845.
  6. Inboxes needed at 30 cold emails per inbox per day: 845 ÷ 30 = 28.2, so round up to 29 inboxes. (Our MATH guide shows how to set those up and warm them.)

Step 5: Make it a daily habit

Why: the reel's point is that 29 calls a month is a habit, not a miracle. The habit is a short list of things done every working day, not a big effort once a month. How:

  • Send your daily number, every working day. Same volume Monday to Friday. No catching up with a giant batch on Friday.
  • Answer every reply the same day. An interested reply that waits two days often goes cold.
  • Offer a specific time in your answer so booking takes one step, not five emails back and forth.
  • Log each booked call in one place, with the date it was booked.
  • On Friday, check the pace table. On track? Change nothing. Behind? Use the problem finder below.

Common mistakes

  • Using 4 weeks instead of 4.33 to get your monthly volume. 4,226 emails a week × 4 = 16,904, but the month really needs 18,299. That's a gap of about 1,400 emails, or roughly 2 calls, every month.
  • Planning with somebody else's emails per call. Use benchmarks only for the first plan, then switch to your own numbers.
  • Sending in bursts. Volume that jumps up and down hurts deliverability and replies.
  • Panicking in week 1. Follow-ups haven't gone out yet. Check pace from week 3.
  • Counting replies, not calls. The goal is booked calls on the calendar. Track that number.

Quick answers

Why 4.33 weeks and not 4?

A year has 52 weeks and 12 months, and 52 ÷ 12 = 4.33. If you divide by 4, your weekly target looks a little higher than you need, and your monthly plan stops matching your calendar.

Should I count weekends?

For B2B, plan on working days (21.65 a month). The reel's "1 call a day" counts all 7 days, which is the same 29 calls, just spread differently.

I'm behind by mid-month. Should I double my sending?

No. A sudden jump in volume can hurt deliverability. Find which step is leaking with the problem finder, fix that, and keep the daily volume steady.

How long until I hit the pace?

If your inboxes are already warmed up, expect the pace from about week 3, once follow-ups are running. If they're new, add the warm-up and ramp time first (about 5 weeks in our MATH guide).
