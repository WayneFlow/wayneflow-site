Why more volume often means the same meetings

When a campaign is slow, the first idea is usually "send more". But volume only multiplies what is already there. A campaign that books 1 meeting per 10,000 emails will book 5 meetings from 50,000. You just burn 5 times more leads, inboxes and time to get there.

A campaign that books 5 meetings from 2,000 emails is a different story. That one has earned more volume.

This is example math, not a promise. The point: B is 25 times better per email than A (2.5 vs 0.1). Scaling A wastes your list. Scaling B grows your calendar.

The rule: Fix first. Scale second. Never the other way around.

The 3 numbers to read

Count people, not emails. If your sequence has 3 emails, 1,000 people means about 3,000 emails. Use people for all 3 numbers so campaigns with different sequence lengths can be compared.

1. Contacts per positive reply

Formula: people contacted ÷ positive replies. A positive reply is someone who wants to hear more, not "remove me" or an out-of-office. Why it matters: it's the cleanest single number. It shows if the right people like the offer.

2. Reply rate

Formula: all replies ÷ people contacted × 100. Why it matters: a low reply rate usually points to the list, the inbox or the first line. A high reply rate with few positive replies points to the offer. For a benchmark, the Instantly 2026 Cold Email Benchmark Report puts the average reply rate at 3.43%, the top 25% at 5.5%+ and the top 10% at 10.7%+.

3. Meetings per 1,000 contacts

Formula: meetings booked ÷ people contacted × 1,000. Why it matters: this is the number you actually get paid on. If positive replies are good but meetings are low, the problem is how you handle replies, not the email.

How to fix without fooling yourself

  1. Pick one thing to change: the message, the targeting or the offer. Only one.
  2. Keep everything else the same: same list source, same inboxes, same send times.
  3. Send to a fresh group of people of similar size. Don't re-send to people who already got the old version.
  4. Wait for enough data. A good rule of thumb: at least 500 to 1,000 people per version and about 7 days for late replies.
  5. Score it again with the same 3 numbers. Better? Keep it. Not better? Undo it and try the next change.

Why one at a time: if you change the subject line, the offer and the list in the same week and results jump, you don't know which one did it. Next time, you can't repeat it.

How to scale a winner safely

  • Grow in steps. For example, double the daily volume, then wait a week and check the scorecard again.
  • Add new people from the same type of list. A winner on US dental clinics is not proof it works on UK law firms.
  • Add inboxes, don't push old ones harder. More emails per inbox can hurt deliverability. Our REALITY guide has the inbox math.
  • Re-check every week. If a number slides into the red, stop adding volume and go back to fixing.

Quick answers

What counts as a positive reply?

Anyone who wants to hear more: "tell me more", "send it over", "what does it cost?". Not out-of-office, not "remove me", not "wrong person" unless they point you to the right one.

How long should I wait before scoring a campaign?

A good rule of thumb is at least 500 to 1,000 people and about 7 days after the last email, so late replies are counted.

My reply rate is great but meetings are low. Should I scale?

Not yet. Fix how you handle replies first: answer fast and offer one clear time. Then scale.

Are these targets the same for every industry?

No. They are starting points. If your deals are very large, fewer meetings can still be a great result. Compare your campaigns against each other, too.
