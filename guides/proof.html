The short answer

A case study only works if the reader sees themselves in it. "We helped a SaaS startup" means nothing to a roofer. He skips it. Worse, it tells him you don't know who he is. So don't add more proof. Add proof from their world.

The fix has four steps: split your list by industry, write one proof line for each industry, use the number that industry counts, and if you have no case study yet, give something free instead.

Why the wrong proof hurts

Proof is meant to answer one question: "Has this worked for someone like me?" A result from another industry answers a different question. The reader has to do the work of translating it, and in a cold email nobody does that work. They just delete.

It can also make things worse than no proof at all. A plain email with no proof is just short. An email with the wrong proof shows the reader you sent the same message to everyone. That's the exact thing people hate about cold email. In Hunter's State of Email Outreach 2026, 61% of decision makers named irrelevance as a reason cold emails fail.

The closer the match, the stronger the proof

This isn't only a cold email idea. In a well-known hotel study, Goldstein, Cialdini and Griskevicius (2008) tested towel-reuse cards. The card that said most guests who stayed in this same room reused their towels got 49.3% reuse. The other "most guests do it" cards got 42.8% combined, and the standard environmental card got 37.2% (figures as summarized in a 2014 follow-up paper). Same message, closer group, better result.

A fair note: that 2014 follow-up ran the test again in other hotels and did not find the same effect everywhere. So treat it as a strong hint, not a law. Your own reply data is the real test, and step 5 below shows how to run it.

The cold email numbers point the same way. Hunter found sequences sent to 21 to 50 people got a 6.2% reply rate, compared with 2.4% for sequences sent to 500+ people. Instantly's 2026 benchmark report puts the average reply rate at 3.43%, and says its top 10% of senders (10.7%+ replies) use micro-segmentation. Small, matched groups beat one big list.

The rule: Not any proof. Not more proof. Proof from their world.

Step 1: Split the list by industry

Why it works: you can't match proof to a reader if all your readers sit in one big list. Once the list is split, every email in a group can speak to the same world.

  1. Export your list with the industry column (Apollo, Clay, LinkedIn Sales Navigator and most data tools give you one).
  2. Clean the labels. "Roofing", "Roofing contractor" and "Roofers" are one group. Put each lead in one folder only.
  3. Keep groups that have at least a few hundred people. Merge tiny groups into the closest real neighbor, or park them for later.
  4. Name each campaign by industry, like "Roofers - Texas" or "Dentists - multi-location", so results are easy to compare.
  5. If you sell to big markets, split again by size or role. A 3-chair dental office and a 40-location group are not the same reader.

Step 2: Write one proof line for each industry

Why it works: one sentence is all the space proof gets in a cold email. If that sentence names their industry, the reader keeps reading. If it doesn't, you lost them on line two.

How: one line per industry, with three parts: who (a company like theirs), what (the result), and how fast. Keep it under 25 words. Put it right after your opening line.

PartWeakStrong
Who"a client""a 6-truck HVAC company in Phoenix"
What"great results""[number] booked service calls"
How fast(missing)"in the first 30 days"

You don't need a client's name. "A roofing company in Dallas" is close enough, and it's safer if you don't have permission to use the name.

Step 3: Use their metric

Why it works: every industry keeps score with its own number. A dentist doesn't care about "leads". She cares about booked appointments. If your proof uses the wrong number, it's still the wrong world, even with the right industry name on it.

How: before you write the line, ask "What number does this owner check every week?" Then rewrite your result in that unit. Here are five industries to start with.

Only real numbers: Use a result you can back up. Never round up, never borrow another company's number, and never invent a client. One wrong number found out later costs more than having no proof.

Step 4: No case study yet? Give something free

Why it works: proof exists to lower risk. If you don't have a result in their industry yet, you can lower the risk a different way: let them see your work before they pay anything.

How: offer one small, specific thing you can make in under an hour. Make it about their business, not yours.

Worked example: same proof, their world

Say your only result so far is this: a SaaS startup booked 40 demos in 60 days with your help. You want to email roofers, HVAC owners and dentists. Your list has 3,000 people.

  1. Split the list: 1,200 roofers, 1,000 HVAC companies, 800 dental offices. That's 3,000 people in three campaigns.
  2. Check what you can honestly claim: you have a SaaS result, not a roofing one. So for the three new industries, the SaaS line can't be your lead proof.
  3. Translate the skill, not the number: the real skill is booking meetings from cold outreach. For roofers that means inspections, for HVAC service calls, for dentists appointments.
  4. Use step 4 for now: each campaign offers a free audit in their own terms. Roofers get a quote-page check. HVAC gets a speed-to-quote test. Dentists get a booking-page check.
  5. Keep the SaaS proof for SaaS readers: "We helped a SaaS startup book 40 demos in 60 days" goes only to the SaaS list, where it's a perfect match.
  6. Swap in real proof as you earn it: the first roofer who gets a result becomes the proof line for the roofer campaign.

Step 5: Check it with your own numbers

Run each industry as its own campaign so you can see the result per group. Test one change at a time: matched proof against no proof, or proof line A against proof line B. Give each version a few hundred sends before you decide. Keep the winner, then test the next idea.

Common mistakes

  • Using one case study for every industry because it's your best one.
  • Listing five logos instead of one matched result.
  • Using your number (leads, MQLs) instead of theirs (appointments, jobs, demos).
  • Naming a client without permission.
  • Offering a free audit that's really a sales call. Give the actual thing.
  • Leaving tiny industry groups in one mixed "other" campaign with generic proof.

The proof-matching prompt

Proof lines for five industries (copy it)
I sell: [what you sell, one line]
My real results (only use these, never invent numbers):
- [result 1: type of client, result, timeframe]
- [result 2, if any]

Write proof lines for these five industries: dentists, HVAC companies, roofers, accounting firms, B2B software (SaaS).

For each industry:
1) The number that owner tracks every week (their metric).
2) If one of my real results matches this industry, write one proof line under 25 words: who, what result in their metric, how fast. Use placeholders like [X] if a number is missing. Never make up a result.
3) If none of my results match, say "no match" and instead write one free audit offer and one free sample offer, each in one sentence, about their business.
4) Words to avoid for this industry.

Plain English, short sentences, no hype. Output as a table.

Quick answers

Is it really worse to use proof from another industry than none?

It often is, because it shows the reader you didn't think about who they are. Don't take anyone's word for it, though. Test matched proof against no proof in your own campaigns and keep what gets more replies.

What if I only have one case study?

Use it only for readers in that industry. For everyone else, offer a free audit or sample (step 4) until you earn a result in their world.

How close does the match need to be?

Same industry is the minimum. Same industry plus similar size or city is better. Their metric in the line matters as much as their industry name.

Can I name the client in the proof line?

Only with their permission. Otherwise describe them, like "a 6-truck HVAC company in Phoenix". That's specific enough to feel real.
