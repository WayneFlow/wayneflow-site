Why "Can we hop on a call?" fails

A meeting request asks a stranger for half an hour of their day, before they even know if you can help. Most people say nothing. Silence is easier than no.

The data agrees. In The Ultimate Cold Email Data Report by Gong, 30 Minutes to President's Club and Jason Bay (85M+ cold emails), asking for a meeting cut reply rates by 44%, and making an offer lifted reply rates by 28%. The report's advice: ask for interest, or make an offer like a free trial, a data report or an audit.

Why small asks work: "Worth a look?" can be answered with one word. The reader doesn't have to check their calendar or commit to anything. They only have to say yes to learning more.

Pick one: Use only one ask per email. Two asks means the reader has to choose, and choosing is work.

The 4-step offer play

Step 1: Offer something first

Can I send the playbook we used for a firm like yours?

Why: you give before you take. The reader gets something useful even if they never buy. That's what makes the offer easy to say yes to.

Step 2: Wait for the yes

Don't attach the playbook to the first email. Don't add a link. Let them reply "sure" or "yes, send it". A reply is the goal of the first email. It also means they asked for what you send next, so it isn't cold anymore.

Step 3: Send it, then offer one time to walk through it

Send what you promised, right away. Then offer one specific time, not a calendar link with 40 slots. One time is a simple yes or no.

After-the-yes message
Hi [first name],

Here it is: [link or attachment].

The part most [role]s find useful is [section or page], where [one-line summary].

Happy to walk you through how it would work for [company]. Does [day] at [time] work for 15 minutes?

[your name]

Step 4: Make it worth a look

The ask only works if the thing you offer is actually worth having. A "free consultation" is a sales call with a nicer name, and everyone knows it. The exact campaign you ran for a firm like theirs is real value.

Quick answers

When should I ask for the meeting?

After the yes. Once they've said "sure, send it", send what you promised and offer one specific time to walk them through it.

What if I don't have a playbook or case study yet?

Offer something small you can make in an hour: a checklist, 3 ideas for their company, or a short audit. Never offer something you don't have.

Does "Worth a look?" work in every industry?

It's a good start almost everywhere because it's so easy to answer. Test it against one offer-style ask and keep the one that gets more replies.

Can I use more than one ask in a sequence?

Yes. Use a different small ask in each follow-up. Just keep one ask per email.
